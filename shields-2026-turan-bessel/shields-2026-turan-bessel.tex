\begin{abstract}
Order log-concavity and the argument Tur\'an inequality are two classical
positivity directions for modified Bessel functions.  We prove that they admit
a sharp matrix coupling: the symmetric matrix formed from the order curvature,
the mixed order--argument derivative, and the classical Tur\'anian is positive
definite throughout the full range \(\nu>-1\), \(z>0\), after a trigamma
correction forced by the vacuum limit.  Passing to a reciprocal-gamma series
identifies the coefficient matrices with Schur deficits of conditioned discrete
Bessel ensembles; mixed-determinant polarization then localizes the endpoint
defect to one fiber and one determinant degree.
This representation separates coefficientwise and pointwise positivity into
microcanonical and canonical phase boundaries controlled by the vacuum,
one-particle, and thermodynamic sectors.  Equivalence of ensembles then matches
the high-degree coefficient walls with the large-argument Bessel boundary,
with cubic agreement at a unique critical point.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Tur\'an inequalities for modified Bessel functions appear as inequalities
between neighboring orders, monotonicity of consecutive-order ratios, and
bounds for logarithmic derivatives
\cite{Baricz2010TuranBessel,BariczPonnusamy2013TuranBessel,Watson1944TreatiseBessel}.
A complementary literature develops log-concavity and coefficientwise Tur\'an
inequalities for hypergeometric and reciprocal-gamma series
\cite{KalmykovKarp2013Reciprocal,KalmykovKarp2013Ratios,KalmykovKarp2014LogConcavity,KarpSitnik2010LogConvexity,KarpZhang2024LogConcavity}.
Two scalar directions are therefore already understood.  The order curvature
is positive because \(I_\nu\) is strictly log-concave in \(\nu\), while the
argument direction below is a classical shifted modified-Bessel Tur\'anian.
The question here is whether these directions admit a genuinely mixed matrix
coupling.

\subsection{Main result}\label{subsec:intro-main}

Put \(\Thetaz=z\partial_z\) and
\[
 G_\nu=-\partial_\nu^2\log I_\nu,\qquad
 P_\nu=\partial_\nu(\Thetaz\log I_\nu),\qquad
 H_\nu=2(\Thetaz\log I_\nu-\nu)-\Thetaz^2\log I_\nu.
\]
The new object is the \emph{Bessel--Schur matrix}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:intro-schur-matrix}
 \mathcal S_\nu(z)=
 \begin{pmatrix}
  G_\nu(z)&1+P_\nu(z)\\
  1+P_\nu(z)&H_\nu(z)+4/\psi_1(\nu+1)
 \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
We prove that \(\mathcal S_\nu(z)\succ0\) for every \(\nu>-1\) and \(z>0\).
The diagonal inequalities themselves are classical; the new statement is the
strict Schur coupling through the mixed order--argument derivative.  The
trigamma correction is forced by the vacuum boundary: as \(z\downarrow0\),
the matrix tends to a rank-one matrix, and changing the correction by a scalar
factor moves its limiting determinant through zero.  Thus the endpoint is not
an arbitrary strengthening of two scalar inequalities but the unique closure
of their mixed Schur deficit.  \Cref{thm:bessel,prop:bessel-sharpness} state
the inequality and its minimality; \Cref{sec:scalar} records the classical
scalar identifications and makes this separation precise.

\subsection{Fixed-total fibers and the three-resolution hierarchy}\label{subsec:intro-hierarchy}

The proof is most naturally carried out after passing to the reciprocal-gamma series
\[
 Z(a,\lambda)=\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{\lambda^k}{k!\,\Gamma(a+k)}
 =\frac{{}_0F_1(;a;\lambda)}{\Gamma(a)},
 \qquad a=\nu+1,\quad z=2\sqrt\lambda.
\]
Parameter and Euler derivatives of \(Z\) form a two-parameter matrix
\(\Turan_{\kappa,\tau}\) whose determinant is carried to the Bessel determinant
by a positive diagonal congruence.  A reciprocal-gamma convolution decomposes
this matrix into fixed-degree fibers \(M_m^{(\kappa,\tau)}\).  At the endpoint,
\(M_0\) has rank one, every \(M_m\) with \(m\ge2\) is positive definite, and
\(M_1\) is the only fiber that can be indefinite.  Mixed-determinant
polarization then localizes its self-interaction to determinant degree two.
The infinite coefficientwise problem therefore reduces to one finite defect.

The same decomposition has a probabilistic meaning that organizes the paper.
The fibers are Schur-deficit matrices for two independent discrete Bessel
counts conditioned on their total; determinant coefficients are obtained by
conditioning four copies on their grand total; and the pointwise Bessel
determinant is the canonical average of these fixed-total sectors.  A single
Schur deficit is therefore being viewed at three resolutions:
\[
 \text{two-copy fiber}\ \longrightarrow\
 \text{four-copy determinant sector}\ \longrightarrow\
 \text{canonical Bessel determinant}.
\]
In particular, coefficientwise and pointwise positivity are respectively the
microcanonical and canonical positivity problems for the same ensemble.

\subsection{Phase geometry and thermodynamic matching}\label{subsec:intro-phase}

The two deformation parameters expose the distinction between these positivity
notions.  The vacuum sector gives the pointwise boundary \(\tau=1\), the
one-particle sector gives the exposed positive-degree coefficient boundary
\(\tau=\tau_{\mathrm{cw}}(a,\kappa)\), and the thermodynamic sector gives the
deformation boundary \(\kappa=1\).  These are the zero-, one-, and
infinite-particle contacts of one sector family.  Between the coefficientwise
and pointwise boundaries, every positive microcanonical sector is nonnegative
while the vacuum sector is negative, and the canonical determinant has exactly
one positive zero.

The individual coefficient walls form an affine fan near the thermodynamic
contact.  On the canonical side, the four-copy total concentrates at
\(N\sim2z\).  The usual coefficient saddle equation is exactly the equation
that tunes the canonical ensemble to a prescribed total, so the agreement
between high-degree coefficient asymptotics and the large-argument Bessel
asymptotics is equivalence of ensembles rather than a separate saddle-point
coincidence.  The matching persists through successive critical cancellations
and becomes cubic at a unique point of the parameter family.

\section{Reciprocal-gamma formulation and positivity phase diagram}\label{sec:main}

Throughout, \(a>0\), \(\lambda\ge0\), and \(\N_0=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}\).  Put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Zdef}
 Z(a,\lambda)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^k}{k!\,\Gamma(a+k)}
 =\frac{{}_0F_1(;a;\lambda)}{\Gamma(a)}.
\end{equation}
We first record the analyticity needed here and in \Cref{app:continuation}.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:continuation}
The series \eqref{eq:Zdef} and all of its fixed-order parameter and Euler
derivatives converge locally uniformly on \(\mathbb C^2\).  Hence \(Z\) and
those derivatives are entire in \((a,\lambda)\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Each summand of \eqref{eq:Zdef} is entire in \(a\).  Uniform Stirling estimates
for \(a\) in compact sets, together with Cauchy estimates for derivatives of
\(1/\Gamma\), give locally uniform convergence of the series and of every
fixed-order parameter and Euler derivative on compact subsets of
\(\mathbb C^2\).
\end{proof}

Write \(\Thetal=\lambda\partial_\lambda\), \(g=\psi_1(a)\), and
\(\Thetaz=z\partial_z\).

For \(\kappa,\tau\in\R\), define
\begin{align}
 A&=Z_a^2-ZZ_{aa},\label{eq:Adef}\\
 B&=Z^2+ZZ_{a\Thetal}-Z_aZ_{\Thetal},\label{eq:Bdef}\\
 C_{\kappa,\tau}
 &=\tau Z^2+g\bigl(\kappa ZZ_{\Thetal}-ZZ_{\Thetal\Thetal}+Z_{\Thetal}^2\bigr),
 \label{eq:Ckt-def}
\end{align}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Tkt}
 \Turan_{\kappa,\tau}
 =\begin{pmatrix}A&\sqrt g\,B\\ \sqrt g\,B&C_{\kappa,\tau}\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 \Delta^{(\kappa,\tau)}=\det\Turan_{\kappa,\tau}.
\end{equation}
By a \emph{matrix Tur\'an determinant} we mean this confluent determinant
assembled from parameter and Euler derivatives, rather than a determinant of
parameter-shifted evaluations; the distinction from classical Tur\'an and
total-positivity constructions is discussed in \Cref{sec:context}.
On \(\tau=1\), write \(C_\kappa,\Turan_\kappa,\Delta^{(\kappa)}\); at
\((\kappa,\tau)=(1,1)\), write \(C,\Turan,\Delta\).

\begin{remark}\label{rem:canonical-origin}
Writing \(L=\log Z\),
\[
 \frac{A}{Z^2}=-L_{aa},\qquad
 \frac{B}{Z^2}=1+L_{a\Thetal},\qquad
 \frac{C_{\kappa,\tau}}{gZ^2}
 =\frac\tau g+\kappa L_{\Thetal}-L_{\Thetal\Thetal}.
\]
Thus \(\kappa\) deforms the Euler direction and \(\tau\) varies an independent
Schur correction.
\end{remark}

Set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:PQdef}
 P=AZ^2,\qquad Q=gA\,ZZ_{\Thetal}.
\end{equation}
At determinant level the deformations are affine:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:affine-two-param}
 \Delta^{(\kappa,\tau)}
 =\Delta+(\kappa-1)Q+(\tau-1)P.
\end{equation}
Both \(P\) and \(Q\) have strictly positive coefficients in every positive
degree.

A function on \((0,\infty)\) is \emph{strictly absolutely monotone} if it and
all its derivatives are strictly positive there.

\begin{theorem}[Sharp endpoint coefficientwise classification]\label{thm:coefficientwise}
For each \(a>0\),
\[
 \Delta^{(\kappa)}(a,\lambda)
 =\sum_{n\ge1}\Delta_n^{(\kappa)}(a)\lambda^n,
 \qquad
 \Delta_n^{(\kappa)}(a)>0\quad(n\ge1),
\]
holds if and only if \(\kappa\ge1\).  Consequently
\(\Delta^{(\kappa)}(a,\cdot)\) is strictly absolutely monotone and
\(\Turan_\kappa(a,\lambda)\succ0\) for \(\kappa\ge1\), \(\lambda>0\).
\end{theorem}

The constant coefficient satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Delta0-tau}
 [\lambda^0]\Delta^{(\kappa,\tau)}
 =\frac{g(\tau-1)}{\Gamma(a)^4}.
\end{equation}
Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tau-cw}
 \tau_{\mathrm{cw}}(a,\kappa)
 =\frac{ag(2a-1)-\frac12(\kappa-1)a^2g^2}{2a^2g-1}.
\end{equation}
The denominator is positive because
\(a^2g=1+a^2\sum_{r\ge1}(a+r)^{-2}>1\).

\begin{theorem}[Two-parameter coefficientwise phase diagram]\label{thm:two-parameter-coeff}
Fix \(a>0\).  All positive-degree coefficients of
\(\Delta^{(\kappa,\tau)}(a,\lambda)\) are nonnegative if and only if
\[
 \kappa\ge1,\qquad \tau\ge\tau_{\mathrm{cw}}(a,\kappa).
\]
They are all strictly positive if and only if
\[
 \kappa\ge1,\qquad \tau>\tau_{\mathrm{cw}}(a,\kappa).
\]
On the affine boundary \(\tau=\tau_{\mathrm{cw}}(a,\kappa)\), the degree-one
coefficient vanishes and every coefficient of degree \(n\ge2\) is strictly
positive.  Moreover \(\tau_{\mathrm{cw}}(a,\kappa)<1\) for every \(\kappa\ge1\).
\end{theorem}

The Bessel form follows from the standard identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:I-Z}
 I_{a-1}(2\sqrt\lambda)=\lambda^{(a-1)/2}Z(a,\lambda)
\end{equation}
\cite[eq.~10.25.2]{NISTDLMFBessel},\,\cite[\S3.7]{Watson1944TreatiseBessel}.
Put \(a=\nu+1\), \(z=2\sqrt\lambda\), and define
\begin{align}
 G_\nu&=-\partial_\nu^2\log I_\nu,\label{eq:Gnu}\\
 P_\nu&=\partial_\nu(\Thetaz\log I_\nu),\label{eq:Pnu}\\
 H_\nu^{(\kappa)}
 &=2\kappa(\Thetaz\log I_\nu-\nu)-\Thetaz^2\log I_\nu.\label{eq:Hnu-kappa}
\end{align}
Throughout, a subscript \(\nu\) marks a Bessel-side quantity: \(P_\nu\) is not
\(P\), and the ratio \(R_\nu\) of \Cref{sec:scalar} is not the pair total \(R\)
of \Cref{sec:coefficients}.
At \(\kappa=1\), write \(H_\nu=H_\nu^{(1)}\).  Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Dnu-kt-def}
 D_\nu^{(\kappa,\tau)}
 =G_\nu\left(H_\nu^{(\kappa)}+\frac{4\tau}{\psi_1(\nu+1)}\right)
 -(1+P_\nu)^2.
\end{equation}
At the endpoint write
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Dnu-def}
 D_\nu=D_\nu^{(1,1)}
 =G_\nu\left(H_\nu+\frac4{\psi_1(\nu+1)}\right)-(1+P_\nu)^2.
\end{equation}

The identity \eqref{eq:I-Z} gives the differential correspondence needed
to transfer the reciprocal-gamma determinant to the Bessel side.  With
\(L=\log Z\), \(z=2\sqrt\lambda\), \(\nu=a-1\), and
\(\Thetaz=2\Thetal\),
\begin{align}
 \Thetaz\log I_\nu&=\nu+2L_{\Thetal}, &
 P_\nu&=1+2L_{a\Thetal},\label{eq:U-L}\\
 G_\nu&=-L_{aa}, &
 H_\nu^{(\kappa)}&=4\kappa L_{\Thetal}-4L_{\Thetal\Thetal}.\label{eq:G-L}
\end{align}
Together with \Cref{rem:canonical-origin}, this gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bessel-congruence}
 \begin{pmatrix}
  G_\nu&1+P_\nu\\
  1+P_\nu&H_\nu^{(\kappa)}+4\tau/g
 \end{pmatrix}
 =\frac1{Z^2}
 \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&2/\sqrt g\end{pmatrix}
 \Turan_{\kappa,\tau}(a,\lambda)
 \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&2/\sqrt g\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Taking determinants yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Dkappa-tau-Delta}
 D_{a-1}^{(\kappa,\tau)}(2\sqrt\lambda)
 =\frac{4\Delta^{(\kappa,\tau)}(a,\lambda)}{gZ(a,\lambda)^4}.
\end{equation}
At \((\kappa,\tau)=(1,1)\), one also has
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ABC-log}
 \frac A{Z^2}=-L_{aa},\qquad
 \frac B{Z^2}=1+L_{a\Thetal},\qquad
 \frac C{gZ^2}=\frac1g+L_{\Thetal}-L_{\Thetal\Thetal},
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:D-Delta}
 D_{a-1}(2\sqrt\lambda)=\frac{4\Delta(a,\lambda)}{gZ(a,\lambda)^4}.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Mixed Bessel--Schur inequality]\label{thm:bessel}
For \(\nu>-1\) and \(z>0\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bessel-main}
 G_\nu(z)\left(H_\nu(z)+\frac4{\psi_1(\nu+1)}\right)>(1+P_\nu(z))^2.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:bessel-matrix}
For \(\nu>-1\), \(z>0\),
\[
 \begin{pmatrix}
 G_\nu(z)&1+P_\nu(z)\\
 1+P_\nu(z)&H_\nu(z)+4/\psi_1(\nu+1)
 \end{pmatrix}\succ0.
\]
As \(z\downarrow0\), it extends continuously to the rank-one matrix
\(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}g&2\\2&4/g\end{smallmatrix}\right)\).
\end{corollary}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:bessel-sharpness}
Fix \(\nu>-1\), put \(a=\nu+1\), and let \(\kappa,\tau\in\R\).  Then
\(D_\nu^{(\kappa,\tau)}(z)>0\) for every \(z>0\) if and only if
\[
 \kappa\ge1,\qquad \tau\ge1.
\]
Equivalently, the associated Bessel--Schur matrix is positive definite for all
\(z>0\) exactly in this quadrant.  If \(\kappa\ge1\) and
\(\tau_{\mathrm{cw}}(a,\kappa)\le\tau<1\), then
\(D_\nu^{(\kappa,\tau)}\) has exactly one zero on \((0,\infty)\).
\end{proposition}

For the critical scaling, put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qndef}
 q_n(a)=[\lambda^n]Q(a,\lambda)>0,
 \qquad
 \kappa_n(a)=1-\frac{\Delta_n(a)}{q_n(a)},
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:c-critical}
 c(a)=\frac4{\psi_1(a)}-4a+\frac72
 =\frac4{\psi_1(\nu+1)}-4\nu-\frac12.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:critical-scaling}
For every fixed \(a>0\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kappa-n-two-term}
 1-\kappa_n(a)
 =\frac{c(a)}n
 +\frac{\frac1{12}-\frac2{\psi_1(a)}}{n^2}
 +O_a(n^{-3}).
\end{equation}
The estimate is uniform for \(a\) in every compact subinterval of
\((0,\infty)\).  Moreover
\begin{equation}\label{eq:critical-constant-match}
 c(a)
 =\lim_{z\to\infty}zD_{a-1}(z)
 =\lim_{n\to\infty}n\bigl(1-\kappa_n(a)\bigr),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:c-range}
 \frac32<c(a)<\frac72,
\end{equation}
with \(c(a)\) strictly decreasing from \(7/2\) to \(3/2\) as \(a\) runs from
\(0\) to \(\infty\), and \(\kappa_n(a)\) is eventually strictly increasing.  For \(\kappa<1\), define
\[
 N_a(\kappa)=\min\{n\ge1:\Delta_n^{(\kappa)}(a)<0\}.
\]
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:first-negative-asymptotic}
 N_a(\kappa)\sim\frac{c(a)}{1-\kappa}
 \qquad(\kappa\uparrow1).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

For the full deformation wall, put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kappa-n-tau-def}
 p_n(a)=[\lambda^n]P(a,\lambda),
 \qquad
 \kappa_{n,\tau}(a)
 =1-\frac{\Delta_n(a)+(\tau-1)p_n(a)}{q_n(a)}.
\end{equation}
For \(n\ge1\), the same affine coefficient defines the individual degree wall
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coefficient-wall}
 \tau_n(a,\kappa)
 =1-\frac{\Delta_n(a)+(\kappa-1)q_n(a)}{p_n(a)}.
\end{equation}
Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:c-d-tau}
 c_\tau(a)=\frac{4\tau}{g}-4a+\frac72,
 \qquad
 d_\tau(a)=\frac1{12}-\frac{2\tau}{g},
 \qquad g=\psi_1(a).
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:wall-fan}
For fixed \(a>0\) and \(\tau\in\R\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vertical-wall-two-term}
 1-\kappa_{n,\tau}(a)
 =\frac{c_\tau(a)}n+\frac{d_\tau(a)}{n^2}+O_{a,\tau}(n^{-3}).
\end{equation}
The estimate is locally uniform in \((a,\tau)\in(0,\infty)\times\R\).  On the
Bessel side,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:D-wall-two-term}
 D_{a-1}^{(1,\tau)}(z)
 =\frac{c_\tau(a)}z
 +\frac{e_\tau(a)}{z^2}
 +O_{a,\tau}(z^{-3}),
 \qquad
 e_\tau(a)=\frac{2\tau}{g}-3(a-1)-\frac13.
\end{equation}
The high-degree walls accumulate at
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tau-infty}
 \tau_\infty(a)=g\left(a-\frac78\right),
\end{equation}
for which \(c_{\tau_\infty}=0\) and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:second-order-match}
 1-\kappa_{n,\tau_\infty}(a)
 =\frac{2(11/12-a)}{n^2}+O_a(n^{-3}),
 \qquad
 D_{a-1}^{(1,\tau_\infty)}(z)
 =\frac{11/12-a}{z^2}+O_a(z^{-3}).
\end{equation}
Moreover
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wall-ordering}
 \tau_\infty(a)<\tau_{\mathrm{cw}}(a,1)<1.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Cubic wall-fan multicritical point]\label{thm:cubic-multicritical}
Let
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cubic-point}
 a_{\mathrm{mc}}=\frac{11}{12},
 \qquad
 \nu_{\mathrm{mc}}=-\frac1{12},
 \qquad
 g_{\mathrm{mc}}=\psi_1(11/12),
 \qquad
 \tau_{\mathrm{mc}}=\frac{g_{\mathrm{mc}}}{24}.
\end{equation}
Then the first two wall jets vanish and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cubic-coeff-scaling}
 1-\kappa_{n,\tau_{\mathrm{mc}}}(a_{\mathrm{mc}})
 =\frac{3}{8n^3}+O(n^{-4}),
\end{equation}
while
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cubic-bessel-scaling}
 D_{\nu_{\mathrm{mc}}}^{(1,\tau_{\mathrm{mc}})}(z)
 =\frac{3}{32z^3}+O(z^{-4}).
\end{equation}
Thus the coefficient--argument matching persists through the third critical
jet under \(n\sim2z\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:wall-orientation}
At \(\kappa=1\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wall-orientation}
 \tau_n(a,1)
 =\tau_\infty(a)+\frac{g}{2n}\left(a-\frac{11}{12}\right)+O_a(n^{-2}).
\end{equation}
Thus the high-degree walls approach \(\tau_\infty(a)\) eventually from below
when \(a<11/12\) and from above when \(a>11/12\).  At the switching value,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:critical-wall-orientation}
 \tau_n(11/12,1)
 =\tau_{\mathrm{mc}}-\frac{3g_{\mathrm{mc}}}{32n^2}+O(n^{-3}).
\end{equation}
For fixed \(\kappa\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wall-transverse}
 \tau_n(a,\kappa)
 =\tau_\infty(a)-\frac g4(\kappa-1)n+O_{a,\kappa}(1),
\end{equation}
so the wall heights tend to \(-\infty\), \(\tau_\infty(a)\), or \(+\infty\)
according as \(\kappa>1\), \(\kappa=1\), or \(\kappa<1\).  More generally,
for every fixed \(x\in\R\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wall-tangent-cone}
 \tau_n\!\left(a,1-\frac{x}{n}\right)
 \longrightarrow \tau_\infty(a)+\frac{gx}{4}.
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\section{Classical scalar Bessel directions}\label{sec:scalar}

The diagonal entries of the Bessel--Schur matrix are classical scalar
quantities, and no positivity result of the present paper is used in
identifying them.

Put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:R-r-def}
 R_\nu(z)=\frac{I_{\nu+1}(z)}{I_\nu(z)},
 \qquad
 r_\nu(z)=zR_\nu(z)=\Thetaz\log I_\nu(z)-\nu.
\end{equation}
Both letters denote first-kind quantities here.\footnote{The capital
\(R_\nu\) agrees with \cite{HornikGrun2013Amos}; the lowercase agrees with
neither of the two works that use it.  Both \cite{Segura2011BoundsRatios} and
\cite{HornikGrun2013Amos} write \(r_\nu\) for \(z^{-1}I_\nu/I_{\nu-1}\), which
is \(R_{\nu-1}/z\) and not the \(zR_\nu\) used here.  In
\cite{Segura2011BoundsRatios}, \(R_\nu\) is in addition the second-kind ratio
\(z^{-1}K_\nu/K_{\nu+1}\).}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:scalar-H}
For \(\nu>-1\), \(z>0\), and \(\kappa\in\R\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:H-r-forms}
 H_\nu^{(\kappa)}
 =r_\nu^2+2(\nu+\kappa)r_\nu-z^2
 =2\kappa r_\nu-zr_\nu'.
\end{equation}
Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{eq:H-Amos-general}
 H_\nu^{(\kappa)}(z)>0
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 R_\nu(z)>
 \frac{z}{\nu+\kappa+\sqrt{(\nu+\kappa)^2+z^2}}.
\end{equation}
At \(\kappa=1\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:H-turan-exact}
 H_\nu(z)
 =z^2R_\nu(z)\bigl(R_\nu(z)-R_{\nu+1}(z)\bigr)
 =z^2\frac{I_{\nu+1}(z)^2-I_\nu(z)I_{\nu+2}(z)}{I_\nu(z)^2}.
\end{equation}
In particular, \(H_\nu>0\) is equivalent both to the shifted modified-Bessel
Tur\'an inequality and to the classical Amos-type lower bound
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Amos-bound-exact}
 R_\nu(z)>
 \frac{z}{\nu+1+\sqrt{(\nu+1)^2+z^2}}.
\end{equation}
Finally,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:H-kappa-global}
 H_\nu^{(\kappa)}(z)>0\ \text{for every }z>0
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \kappa\ge1.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The modified Bessel equation \cite[eq.~10.25.1]{NISTDLMFBessel} gives
\[
 \Thetaz^2\log I_\nu
 =z^2+\nu^2-(\Thetaz\log I_\nu)^2
 =z^2-2\nu r_\nu-r_\nu^2.
\]
Substitution in \eqref{eq:Hnu-kappa} gives the first expression in
\eqref{eq:H-r-forms}; since
\(\Thetaz^2\log I_\nu=\Thetaz r_\nu=zr_\nu'\), the second follows as well.
Since \(I_\nu(z)>0\) for \(\nu>-1\) and \(z>0\), one has \(r_\nu>0\).  The
quadratic \(r^2+2(\nu+\kappa)r-z^2\) has one positive and one negative root, so
\(H_\nu^{(\kappa)}>0\) exactly when \(r_\nu\) exceeds the positive root
\[
 \sqrt{(\nu+\kappa)^2+z^2}-(\nu+\kappa)
 =\frac{z^2}{\nu+\kappa+\sqrt{(\nu+\kappa)^2+z^2}},
\]
which proves \eqref{eq:H-Amos-general}.  The recurrence
\(R_{\nu+1}=R_\nu^{-1}-2(\nu+1)/z\)
\cite[\S3.71]{Watson1944TreatiseBessel} gives \eqref{eq:H-turan-exact}.  Positivity at \(\kappa=1\) is the classical
modified-Bessel Tur\'an inequality on
\(\nu>-1\)~\cite[Thm.~2.1]{Baricz2010TuranBessel},\cite[\S1]{BariczPonnusamy2013TuranBessel};
equivalently it is
the Amos-type quotient inequality \eqref{eq:Amos-bound-exact}
\cite{Amos1974Ratios,HornikGrun2013Amos,Nasell1978Rational,Segura2011BoundsRatios}.
For \(\kappa\ge1\),
\[
 H_\nu^{(\kappa)}=H_\nu+2(\kappa-1)r_\nu>0.
\]
Conversely the ascending series gives
\(r_\nu(z)=z^2/(2(\nu+1))+O(z^4)\), hence
\[
 H_\nu^{(\kappa)}(z)
 =\frac{\kappa-1}{\nu+1}z^2+O(z^4),
\]
which is negative near zero when \(\kappa<1\).  This proves
\eqref{eq:H-kappa-global}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:schur-correction}
The correction \(4/g\) is not required for scalar positivity of the second
diagonal direction: \(H_\nu>0\) already holds by \Cref{prop:scalar-H}.  Its role
is matrix-theoretic.  As \(z\downarrow0\),
\[
 \begin{pmatrix}
  G_\nu&1+P_\nu\\
  1+P_\nu&H_\nu+4\tau/g
 \end{pmatrix}
 \longrightarrow
 \begin{pmatrix}g&2\\2&4\tau/g\end{pmatrix},
\]
whose determinant is \(4(\tau-1)\).  Thus \(4/g=2^2/g\) is exactly the Schur
amount needed to close the mixed deficit at the rank-one boundary.
\end{remark}

\section{Reciprocal-gamma convolution and canonical--microcanonical structure}\label{sec:coefficients}

The coefficient calculation rests on a two-parameter Chu--Vandermonde identity.

\begin{lemma}[Asymmetric reciprocal-gamma convolution]\label{lem:convolution}
Let \(m\in\N_0\) and let \(\alpha,\beta>0\).  Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:asymmetric-convolution}
 \sum_{i=0}^m
 \frac1{i!(m-i)!\Gamma(\alpha+i)\Gamma(\beta+m-i)}
 =\frac{(\alpha+\beta+m-1)_m}
 {m!\Gamma(\alpha+m)\Gamma(\beta+m)}.
\end{equation}
Both sides are entire in \((\alpha,\beta)\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Multiply the left-hand side by \(m!\Gamma(\alpha+m)\Gamma(\beta+m)\).  Using
\[
 \frac{\Gamma(\alpha+m)}{\Gamma(\alpha+i)}=(\alpha+i)_{m-i}
 =\frac{(\alpha)_m}{(\alpha)_i}
\]
and
\[
 \frac{\Gamma(\beta+m)}{\Gamma(\beta+m-i)}
 =(\beta+m-i)_i=(-1)^i(1-\beta-m)_i,
\]
the resulting sum is
\[
 (\alpha)_m
 {}_2F_1(-m,1-\beta-m;\alpha;1).
\]
Chu--Vandermonde gives \((\alpha+\beta+m-1)_m\).  Both sides of
\eqref{eq:asymmetric-convolution} are entire in \((\alpha,\beta)\), being finite
sums and products of the entire function \(1/\Gamma\) and a polynomial, so
agreement on \(\{\alpha,\beta>0\}\) extends the identity to \(\mathbb C^2\) by the
identity theorem in each variable separately.
\end{proof}

Set
\[
 S_m=S_m(a)=\frac{(2a+m-1)_m}{m!\Gamma(a+m)^2}>0.
\]
Taking \(\alpha=\beta=a\) in \Cref{lem:convolution} gives
\([\lambda^m]Z(a,\lambda)^2=S_m\),
where \([\lambda^m]\) extracts the coefficient of \(\lambda^m\).  Unsubscripted
\(\alpha,\beta\) always denote the parameters of \Cref{lem:convolution}; the
subscripted families of \Cref{thm:coefficients} below are coefficient sequences.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:coefficients}
There are expansions
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ABC-expansions}
 A=\sum_{m\ge0}S_m\alpha_m\lambda^m,
 \qquad
 B=\sum_{m\ge0}S_m\beta_m\lambda^m,
 \qquad
 C_{\kappa,\tau}=\sum_{m\ge0}S_m\gamma_m^{(\kappa,\tau)}\lambda^m,
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:alpha}
 \alpha_m=\psi_1(a+m),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:beta}
 \beta_0=1,
 \qquad
 \beta_m=\frac{2a+m-2}{2(a+m-1)}\quad(m\ge1),
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gamma-kappa}
 \gamma_m^{(\kappa,\tau)}=\tau+g c_m^{(\kappa)},
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cm-kappa}
 c_0^{(\kappa)}=0,
 \qquad
 c_1^{(\kappa)}=\frac{\kappa-1}{2},
\end{equation}
and, for \(m\ge2\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cm-kappa-general}
 c_m^{(\kappa)}
 =\frac{\kappa m}{2}
 -\frac{m(2a+m-2)}{2(2a+2m-3)}
 =\frac{m\left[(\kappa-1)(2a+2m-3)+m-1\right]}
 {2(2a+2m-3)}.
\end{equation}
At the endpoint \(\kappa=1\),
\[
 c_0=c_1=0,
 \qquad
 c_m=\frac{m(m-1)}{2(2a+2m-3)}\quad(m\ge2).
\]
Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:matrix-series-general}
 \Turan_{\kappa,\tau}(a,\lambda)
 =\sum_{m\ge0}S_mM_m^{(\kappa,\tau)}\lambda^m,
 \qquad
 M_m^{(\kappa,\tau)}=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 \alpha_m&\sqrt g\,\beta_m\\
 \sqrt g\,\beta_m&\tau+gc_m^{(\kappa)}
 \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
At the endpoint \((\kappa,\tau)=(1,1)\), this becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:matrix-series}
 \Turan(a,\lambda)=\sum_{m\ge0}S_mM_m\lambda^m,
 \qquad
 M_m=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 \alpha_m&\sqrt g\,\beta_m\\
 \sqrt g\,\beta_m&1+gc_m
 \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For the first coefficient, define
\[
 F_m(\delta)=
 \sum_{i=0}^m
 \frac1{i!(m-i)!\Gamma(a+\delta+i)\Gamma(a-\delta+m-i)}.
\]
By \Cref{lem:convolution},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Fdelta}
 F_m(\delta)=
 \frac{(2a+m-1)_m}
 {m!\Gamma(a+m+\delta)\Gamma(a+m-\delta)}.
\end{equation}
On the one hand,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:A-second-delta}
 \left.\partial_\delta^2
 \bigl[Z(a+\delta,\lambda)Z(a-\delta,\lambda)\bigr]
 \right|_{\delta=0}
 =2ZZ_{aa}-2Z_a^2=-2A.
\end{equation}
On the other hand, \(F_m'(0)=0\) and the logarithmic second derivative of
\eqref{eq:Fdelta} is \(-2\psi_1(a+m)\) at \(\delta=0\), so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:F-second-delta}
 F_m''(0)=-2S_m\psi_1(a+m).
\end{equation}
Comparing the coefficient of \(\lambda^m\) in
\eqref{eq:A-second-delta} with \eqref{eq:F-second-delta} gives
\([\lambda^m]A=S_m\psi_1(a+m)\), proving \eqref{eq:alpha}.

For \(m=0\), one has \([\lambda^0]B=Z(a,0)^2=S_0\), so \(\beta_0=1\).
Assume henceforth that \(m\ge1\).  Let \(\mathsf I\) be the index of the first factor in
\(Z(a+\delta,\lambda)Z(a-\delta,\lambda)=\sum_mF_m(\delta)\lambda^m\), so that
\([\lambda^m]\) weights \(i\) by
\[
 w_i(\delta)=\bigl[i!(m-i)!\Gamma(a+\delta+i)\Gamma(a-\delta+m-i)\bigr]^{-1}.
\]
For real \(\delta\) with \(|\delta|<a\), write \(\E_\delta\) for expectation
under the probability weights \(w_i(\delta)/F_m(\delta)\), and put
\(\E=\E_0\), \(\Dfin=m-2\mathsf I\), and \(\mathsf J=m-\mathsf I\).  Applying \Cref{lem:convolution} to
\(\sum_i i\,w_i(\delta)\), with \((\alpha,\beta)=(a+1+\delta,a-\delta)\) and \(m-1\) in
place of \(m\), gives
\[
 \sum_{i=0}^m i\,w_i(\delta)
 =\frac{(2a+m-1)_{m-1}}
 {(m-1)!\,\Gamma(a+m+\delta)\Gamma(a+m-1-\delta)}.
\]
Dividing by \eqref{eq:Fdelta} yields
\[
 \E_\delta \mathsf I=\frac{m(a+m-1-\delta)}{2(a+m-1)},
\]
whence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:EDdelta}
 \E_\delta\Dfin=\frac{m\delta}{a+m-1}.
\end{equation}
The score \(\partial_\delta\log w_i(\delta)\big|_{\delta=0}\) is
\[
 \Xi=\psi(a+m-\mathsf I)-\psi(a+\mathsf I),
\]
so differentiating \eqref{eq:EDdelta} at \(\delta=0\) gives
\(\E(\Dfin \Xi)=\partial_\delta\E_\delta\Dfin|_{\delta=0}\), that is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:EDXi}
 \E(\Dfin \Xi)=\frac{m}{a+m-1}.
\end{equation}
Direct coefficient extraction gives
\begin{align*}
 &[\lambda^m]\bigl(ZZ_{a\Thetal}-Z_aZ_{\Thetal}\bigr)\\
 &\quad=\sum_{i=0}^m
 \frac{-(m-i)\psi(a+m-i)+(m-i)\psi(a+i)}
 {i!\,(m-i)!\,\Gamma(a+i)\Gamma(a+m-i)}
 =-S_m\E(\mathsf J\Xi).
\end{align*}
Together with \([\lambda^m]Z^2=S_m\), this yields
\[
 \frac{[\lambda^m]B}{S_m}=1-\E(\mathsf J\Xi)
 =1-\frac12\E(\Dfin \Xi),
\]
because \(\mathsf J=(m+\Dfin)/2\) and \(\E\Xi=0\) by symmetry.  This proves
\eqref{eq:beta}.

For \(C_{\kappa,\tau}\), the term \(\tau Z^2\) contributes \(\tau\) after normalization by \(S_m\), while the Euler-derivative terms contribute
\[
 \kappa\E \mathsf J-\E \mathsf J^2+\E(\mathsf I\mathsf J).
\]
Since \(\mathsf I=(m-\Dfin)/2\), \(\mathsf J=(m+\Dfin)/2\), and \(\E\Dfin=0\) by the symmetry \(\mathsf I\leftrightarrow m-\mathsf I\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:c-cond-derivation}
 \kappa\E \mathsf J-\E \mathsf J^2+\E(\mathsf I\mathsf J)
 =\frac{\kappa m}{2}-\frac12\E\Dfin^2.
\end{equation}
For \(m\ge2\), canceling the factors \(i(m-i)\) and shifting
\(j=i-1\) gives
\begin{align*}
 S_m(a)\E[\mathsf I(m-\mathsf I)]
 &=\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}
 \frac1{(i-1)!(m-i-1)!\Gamma(a+i)\Gamma(a+m-i)}\\
 &=S_{m-2}(a+1).
\end{align*}
Therefore
\[
 \E[\mathsf I(m-\mathsf I)]
 =\frac{S_{m-2}(a+1)}{S_m(a)}
 =\frac{m(m-1)(a+m-1)}{2(2a+2m-3)},
\]
so
\[
 \E\Dfin^2=\frac{m(2a+m-2)}{2a+2m-3}.
\]
Substitution gives \eqref{eq:cm-kappa-general}; the cases \(m=0,1\) are immediate, and adding the \(\tau Z^2\) contribution proves \eqref{eq:gamma-kappa}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:coefficient-caveat}
\Cref{thm:coefficients} does not assert \(M_m\succeq0\) for every \(m\):
\(M_1\) can be indefinite for small \(a>0\) (see \Cref{subsec:gram}).  The general
coefficient matrix is \(M_m^{(\kappa,\tau)}\) of
\eqref{eq:matrix-series-general}; the endpoint matrix \(M_m\) means
\(M_m^{(1,1)}\).
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:fiber-deformation}
Let \(e_2=(0,1)^T\).  The explicit formula for \(c_m^{(\kappa)}\) gives, for
every \(m\ge0\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fiber-affine}
 M_m^{(\kappa,\tau)}
 =M_m+
 \left[(\tau-1)+\frac{gm}{2}(\kappa-1)\right]e_2e_2^T.
\end{equation}
Thus the vacuum fiber \(m=0\) sees only the correction parameter \(\tau\), the
first nonconstant fiber sees the first affine combination of \(\tau\) and
\(\kappa\), and the \(\kappa\)-deformation is amplified linearly with the
fiber total.  This is the coefficient-level origin of the three phase scales.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Microcanonical Bessel fibers and canonical averaging}\label{subsec:microcanonical}

For \(a>0\), \(\lambda>0\), let \(Y,Y_1,Y_2,\ldots\) be independent random
variables with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bessel-law}
 \mathbb P_\lambda(Y=k)
 =\frac{\lambda^k}{k!\,\Gamma(a+k)Z(a,\lambda)},
 \qquad k\in\N_0.
\end{equation}
Under \(a=\nu+1\), \(z=2\sqrt\lambda\), this is the classical discrete Bessel
law
\[
 \mathbb P(Y=k)
 =\frac{(z/2)^{2k+\nu}}
 {I_\nu(z)\,k!\,\Gamma(k+\nu+1)}
\]
studied by Yuan and Kalbfleisch and by Devroye
\cite{YuanKalbfleisch2000Bessel,Devroye2002Bessel}.

Put \(R=Y_1+Y_2\).  The law \eqref{eq:bessel-law} is a power-series
distribution in the classical sense \cite{Patil1962PowerSeries}; for a modern
account of the correspondence between nonnegative power series and their
associated probability laws and moments, see
\cite{Canton2026PowerSeries}.  Fixed-sum conditional characterizations of
discrete distributions are classical \cite{PatilSeshadri1964Characterization}.
Here the conditioning is used to construct the Bessel Schur-deficit fibers.
For fixed \(m\), let
\(\mathsf I_m\) have the conditional law of \(Y_1\) given \(R=m\), and set
\[
 \Dfin_m=m-2\mathsf I_m,
 \qquad
 \Xi_m=\psi(a+m-\mathsf I_m)-\psi(a+\mathsf I_m),
\]
\[
 \Sigma_m
 =\psi_1(a+\mathsf I_m)+\psi_1(a+m-\mathsf I_m).
\]

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:ensemble-hierarchy}
For every \(m\in\N_0\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pair-total-law}
 \mathbb P_\lambda(R=m)=\frac{S_m\lambda^m}{Z(a,\lambda)^2},
 \qquad
 \mathbb P(\mathsf I_m=i)
 =\frac{1}{S_m\,i!(m-i)!\Gamma(a+i)\Gamma(a+m-i)}.
\end{equation}
The conditional law is independent of \(\lambda\).  Define, near
\((u,v)=(0,0)\),
\[
 \mathcal F_m(u,v)
 =\sum_{i=0}^m
 \frac{\exp\{-v(m-2i)/\sqrt2\}}
 {i!(m-i)!\Gamma(a+u/\sqrt2+i)
  \Gamma(a-u/\sqrt2+m-i)},
 \qquad
 \ell_m=\log\mathcal F_m.
\]
Then the normalized coefficient matrix
\[
 \widehat M_m^{(\kappa,\tau)}
 =\diag(1,g^{-1/2})M_m^{(\kappa,\tau)}\diag(1,g^{-1/2})
\]
has the microcanonical differential form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:microcanonical-schur}
 \widehat M_m^{(\kappa,\tau)}
 =\begin{pmatrix}
  -\ell_{m,uu}&1+\ell_{m,uv}\\
  1+\ell_{m,uv}&\tau/g+\kappa m/2-\ell_{m,vv}
 \end{pmatrix}_{(u,v)=(0,0)}.
\end{equation}
Equivalently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:finite-law-entries}
 \alpha_m=\frac12\E(\Sigma_m-\Xi_m^2),\qquad
 \beta_m=1-\frac12\E(\Dfin_m\Xi_m),\qquad
 c_m^{(\kappa)}=\frac{\kappa m}{2}-\frac12\E\Dfin_m^2.
\end{equation}
Since \(\E\Xi_m=\E\Dfin_m=0\), this is equivalently the microcanonical
covariance-deficit identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:microcanonical-covariance-deficit}
 \widehat M_m^{(\kappa,\tau)}
 =\begin{pmatrix}
   \frac12\E\Sigma_m&1\\
   1&\tau/g+\kappa m/2
  \end{pmatrix}
  -\frac12\operatorname{Cov}\!\begin{pmatrix}\Xi_m\\ \Dfin_m\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The formal structure ``conditional expected curvature minus score covariance''
in \eqref{eq:microcanonical-covariance-deficit} is analogous to the
missing-information identity for observed information in latent-variable
models \cite{Louis1982ObservedInformation}; no identification with that
likelihood framework is asserted here.

Finally, the normalized differential matrix is the canonical average of these
fixed-total fibers:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:canonical-fiber-average}
 \frac1{Z^2}\diag(1,g^{-1/2})
 \Turan_{\kappa,\tau}
 \diag(1,g^{-1/2})
 =\E_\lambda\widehat M_R^{(\kappa,\tau)}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Independence and \([\lambda^m]Z^2=S_m\) give the first identity in
\eqref{eq:pair-total-law}; division by that pair-total probability gives the
conditional law.  Under this law the \(u\)-score of a summand of
\(\mathcal F_m\) at the origin is \(\Xi_m/\sqrt2\), its \(u\)-curvature is
\(-\Sigma_m/2\), and its \(v\)-score is \(-\Dfin_m/\sqrt2\), while its \(v\)-
and mixed curvatures vanish because \(v\) enters the summand linearly in the
exponent and separately from \(u\).  Each second logarithmic derivative of a
positive sum is the mean of the corresponding summandwise curvature plus the
covariance of the two summandwise scores, and \(\E\Xi_m=\E\Dfin_m=0\) by
symmetry, so
\[
 -\ell_{m,uu}=\frac12\E(\Sigma_m-\Xi_m^2),\qquad
 \ell_{m,uv}=-\frac12\E(\Dfin_m\Xi_m),\qquad
 \ell_{m,vv}=\frac12\E\Dfin_m^2.
\]
The identities in \eqref{eq:finite-law-entries} are exactly
\eqref{eq:F-second-delta}, \eqref{eq:EDXi}, and
\eqref{eq:c-cond-derivation}, so \eqref{eq:microcanonical-schur} follows from
\Cref{thm:coefficients}; \eqref{eq:microcanonical-covariance-deficit} is the
same identity grouped into baseline and covariance terms.  Finally, weighting
the coefficient expansion
\eqref{eq:matrix-series-general} by the pair-total law gives
\eqref{eq:canonical-fiber-average}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:bessel-law}
Let \(\Psi=\psi(a+Y)\), \(\mu=\E Y\), and
\(\sigma^2=\operatorname{Var}Y\).  Then
\begin{align}
 L_{\Thetal}&=\mu,&
 L_{\Thetal\Thetal}&=\sigma^2,\label{eq:bessel-law-meanvar}\\
 L_{a\Thetal}&=-\operatorname{Cov}(\Psi,Y),&
 -L_{aa}&=\E\psi_1(a+Y)-\operatorname{Var}\Psi.\label{eq:bessel-law-param}
\end{align}
Consequently \eqref{eq:canonical-fiber-average} is the covariance-deficit
matrix
\begin{equation}\label{eq:covariance-deficit-matrix}
 \begin{pmatrix}
  \E\psi_1(a+Y)-\operatorname{Var}\Psi
  &1-\operatorname{Cov}(\Psi,Y)\\[1mm]
  1-\operatorname{Cov}(\Psi,Y)
  &\tau/g+\kappa\mu-\sigma^2
 \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Equivalently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:covariance-loewner}
 \begin{pmatrix}
  \E\psi_1(a+Y)&1\\
  1&\tau/g+\kappa\mu
 \end{pmatrix}
 -\operatorname{Cov}\!\begin{pmatrix}\Psi\\Y\end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
is the normalized matrix.  Moreover
\begin{equation}\label{eq:H-dispersion}
 H_\nu^{(\kappa)}=4(\kappa\mu-\sigma^2),
\end{equation}
so \(H_\nu>0\) is exactly underdispersion of the Bessel law.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Average \eqref{eq:finite-law-entries} over the independent pair
\((Y_1,Y_2)\).  Since
\[
 \frac12\E\{\psi_1(a+Y_1)+\psi_1(a+Y_2)\}=\E\psi_1(a+Y),
\]
\[
 \frac12\E(\Psi_2-\Psi_1)^2=\operatorname{Var}\Psi,
 \qquad
 \frac12\E\{(Y_2-Y_1)(\Psi_2-\Psi_1)\}
 =\operatorname{Cov}(Y,\Psi),
\]
and
\[
 \frac12\E(Y_2-Y_1)^2=\operatorname{Var}Y,
\]
comparison with \Cref{rem:canonical-origin} proves the derivative and matrix
identities.  Equation \eqref{eq:H-dispersion} follows from
\eqref{eq:G-L}.
\end{proof}

At the endpoint, \Cref{thm:bessel} is therefore equivalent to a covariance
inequality for the discrete Bessel law \eqref{eq:bessel-law}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:covariance-ineq}
 \bigl(1-\operatorname{Cov}(\Psi,Y)\bigr)^2
 <\bigl(\E\psi_1(a+Y)-\operatorname{Var}\Psi\bigr)
 \left(\frac1g+\E Y-\operatorname{Var}Y\right).
\end{equation}

\subsection{Four-copy determinant sectors}\label{subsec:four-copy}

For symmetric \(2\times2\) matrices \(U=(U_{ij})\) and \(V=(V_{ij})\), define
\[
 \MD(U,V)=U_{11}V_{22}+U_{22}V_{11}-2U_{12}V_{12}.
\]
This is the polarization of the determinant; up to the conventional factorial
normalization, it is the mixed discriminant in dimension two
\cite{Bapat1989MixedDiscriminants}.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:MD-positive}
If \(U,V\succeq0\), then \(\MD(U,V)\ge0\).  If in addition \(U\ne0\) and
\(V\succ0\), then \(\MD(U,V)>0\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write \(U=\sum_r u_ru_r^T\) and \(V=\sum_s v_sv_s^T\).  Then
\[
 \MD(U,V)=\sum_{r,s}
 \left(u_{r,1}v_{s,2}-u_{r,2}v_{s,1}\right)^2.
\]
If this sum vanished and \(U\ne0\), every Gram vector of \(V\) would be
parallel to a fixed nonzero Gram vector of \(U\), forcing \(V\) to have rank
at most one.
\end{proof}

Let \(R'=Y_3+Y_4\) and \(N=R+R'\).  Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Tn-Kn-law}
 T_n=\sum_{k=0}^nS_kS_{n-k}=[\lambda^n]Z^4,
 \qquad
 \mathbb P(K_n=k)=\frac{S_kS_{n-k}}{T_n}.
\end{equation}
Thus \(K_n\) has the conditional law of the first pair total \(R\) given
\(N=n\).

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:four-copy}
The canonical total has law
\begin{equation}\label{eq:four-total-law}
 \mathbb P_\lambda(N=n)=\frac{T_n\lambda^n}{Z^4}.
\end{equation}
For every \(n\ge0\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Delta-n-MD}
 \Delta_n^{(\kappa,\tau)}
 =\frac12\sum_{k=0}^nS_kS_{n-k}
 \MD\!\left(M_k^{(\kappa,\tau)},M_{n-k}^{(\kappa,\tau)}\right)
 =\frac{T_n}{2}\E\MD\!\left(
 M_{K_n}^{(\kappa,\tau)},M_{n-K_n}^{(\kappa,\tau)}\right).
\end{equation}
If
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sector-density}
 d_n^{(\kappa,\tau)}
 =\frac{4\Delta_n^{(\kappa,\tau)}}{gT_n},
\end{equation}
then the pointwise Bessel determinant is the canonical average of the
microcanonical sector densities:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:D-canonical-average}
 D_{a-1}^{(\kappa,\tau)}(2\sqrt\lambda)
 =\E_\lambda d_N^{(\kappa,\tau)}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The total-count law follows from \(T_n=[\lambda^n]Z^4\).  Expanding the
determinant of \eqref{eq:matrix-series-general} by polarization gives
\[
 \Delta_n^{(\kappa,\tau)}
 =\frac12\sum_{k=0}^nS_kS_{n-k}
 \MD\!\left(M_k^{(\kappa,\tau)},M_{n-k}^{(\kappa,\tau)}\right),
\]
which is \eqref{eq:Delta-n-MD}.  Finally, combine
\eqref{eq:Dkappa-tau-Delta} with \eqref{eq:four-total-law} and
\eqref{eq:sector-density}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:ensemble-positivity}
Equation \eqref{eq:D-canonical-average} makes the phase separation literal.
Coefficientwise positivity asks for the sign of every positive fixed-total
sector \(d_n\), whereas pointwise positivity asks for the sign of their
canonical mixture for every fugacity \(\lambda\).  The vacuum sector is
\(d_0^{(\kappa,\tau)}=4(\tau-1)\),
so the pointwise correction wall is already visible before any positive-count
sector is populated.
\end{remark}

\section{Endpoint coefficientwise positivity}\label{sec:endpoint}

We require elementary two-sided trigamma estimates.  The series
\(\psi_1(y)=\sum_{r\ge0}(y+r)^{-2}\) is standard
\cite[eq.~5.15.1]{NISTDLMFGamma}; inserting
\((y+r)^{-2}=\int_0^\infty\theta e^{-(y+r)\theta}\dd\theta\) and summing
\(\sum_{r\ge0}e^{-r\theta}=(1-e^{-\theta})^{-1}\) under the integral gives the
integral representation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:trigamma-integral}
 \psi_1(y)=\int_0^\infty e^{-y\theta}\frac{\theta}{1-e^{-\theta}}\dd\theta,
 \qquad y>0.
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:trigamma-bounds}
For every \(y>0\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:trig-lower}
 \psi_1(y)>\frac1y+\frac1{2y^2}>\frac1y.
\end{equation}
For \(y>1/2\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:trig-upper-half}
 \psi_1(y)<\frac1{y-1/2}.
\end{equation}
For every \(y>0\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:trig-upper-cubic}
 \psi_1(y)<\frac1y+\frac1{2y^2}+\frac1{6y^3}.
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inverse-trig}
 \frac1{\psi_1(a)}>a-\frac12
 \qquad(a>0),
\end{equation}
where the assertion is immediate for \(a\le1/2\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For \(h(s)=(y+s)^{-2}\), strict convexity on every interval \([r,r+1]\)
gives the strict trapezoidal inequality
\[
 \int_r^{r+1}h(s)\dd s<\frac{h(r)+h(r+1)}2.
\]
Summing and passing to the limit yields
\[
 \psi_1(y)=\sum_{r\ge0}h(r)>
 \int_0^\infty h(s)\dd s+\frac12h(0)
 =\frac1y+\frac1{2y^2},
\]
which proves \eqref{eq:trig-lower}.  For the first upper bound, the elementary inequality
\(\theta<2\sinh(\theta/2)\) for \(\theta>0\) is equivalent to
\(\theta/(1-e^{-\theta})<e^{\theta/2}\).  Substitution in
\eqref{eq:trigamma-integral} gives \eqref{eq:trig-upper-half}.
For the second, use
\[
 \frac{\theta}{1-e^{-\theta}}
 =\frac\theta2\coth\frac\theta2+\frac\theta2
 <1+\frac\theta2+\frac{\theta^2}{12},
\]
where \(x\coth x<1+x^2/3\) for \(x>0\) because
\[
 (3+x^2)\sinh x-3x\cosh x
 =\sum_{k\ge2}\frac{4k(k-1)}{(2k+1)!}x^{2k+1}>0.
\]
Integrating termwise in
\eqref{eq:trigamma-integral} gives \eqref{eq:trig-upper-cubic}.  Taking
reciprocals in \eqref{eq:trig-upper-half} proves \eqref{eq:inverse-trig} when
\(a>1/2\).
\end{proof}

\subsection{Gram structure and the exceptional matrix \texorpdfstring{\(M_1\)}{M1}}\label{subsec:gram}

Fix \(m\ge1\) and write \(x=a+m\) and \(q=a+m/2-1\).
In \(\ell^2(\N_0)\) define
\[
 u_r^{(m)}=\frac1{x+r},
 \qquad
 v_r^{(m)}=\frac{q}{x+r-1},
 \qquad r\ge0.
\]
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:u-norm}
 \|u^{(m)}\|^2=\psi_1(x)=\alpha_m,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uv-cross}
 \langle u^{(m)},v^{(m)}\rangle
 =q\sum_{r\ge0}\frac1{(x+r-1)(x+r)}
 =\frac q{x-1}=\beta_m,
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:v-norm}
 \|v^{(m)}\|^2=q^2\psi_1(x-1).
\end{equation}
Define, for \(s\in\R\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rho-m}
 \rho_m(s)=s+c_m-q^2\psi_1(x-1),
\end{equation}
and let \(N_m(s)\) be the symmetric matrix with entries \(\alpha_m\), \(\beta_m\) and \(s+c_m\).

For vectors \(\xi,\eta\) in a Hilbert space, \(\operatorname{Gram}(\xi,\eta)\) denotes the symmetric \(2\times2\) matrix with entries \(\langle\xi,\xi\rangle\), \(\langle\xi,\eta\rangle\), \(\langle\eta,\eta\rangle\).  Write \(N_m=N_m(1/g)=\diag(1,g^{-1/2})M_m\diag(1,g^{-1/2})\) for the normalized coefficient matrix.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:gram}
Let \(m\ge1\), \(a>0\) and \(s\in\R\) with \(\rho_m(s)>0\).  In \(\ell^2(\N_0)\oplus\R\), put
\[
 \xi_m=(u^{(m)},0),
 \qquad
 \eta_m(s)=(v^{(m)},\sqrt{\rho_m(s)}).
\]
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Nm-gram}
 N_m(s)=\operatorname{Gram}(\xi_m,\eta_m(s))
\end{equation}
is positive definite.  If either \(m\ge2\) and \(a>0\), or \(m=1\) and \(a\ge1/2\), then \(\rho_m(1/g)>0\), so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Mm-gram}
 M_m=\operatorname{Gram}(\xi_m,\sqrt g\,\eta_m(1/g))
\end{equation}
is positive definite in those ranges.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The Gram identity follows from \eqref{eq:u-norm}--\eqref{eq:rho-m}: the final coordinate contributes \(\rho_m(s)\), so the lower-right entry is \(q^2\psi_1(x-1)+\rho_m(s)=s+c_m\).  In a vanishing combination \(\mu\,\xi_m+\omega\,\eta_m(s)=0\) the last coordinate gives \(\omega\sqrt{\rho_m(s)}=0\), hence \(\omega=0\), and then \(\|u^{(m)}\|^2=\psi_1(x)>0\) forces \(\mu=0\).  The two vectors are linearly independent, so \(N_m(s)\succ0\); the same combination with \(\sqrt g\,\eta_m(1/g)\) gives \eqref{eq:Mm-gram}.

It remains to verify \(\rho_m(1/g)>0\) in the stated ranges.  Assume first that \(m\ge2\).  Then \(x-1>1/2\), so \Cref{lem:trigamma-bounds} gives
\[
 \rho_m(1/g)>
 a-\frac12+c_m-\frac{q^2}{x-3/2}.
\]
The right-hand side simplifies exactly to
\[
 a-\frac12+\frac{m(m-1)}{2(2a+2m-3)}
 -\frac{(a+m/2-1)^2}{a+m-3/2}
 =\frac{m-1}{2(2a+2m-3)}>0.
\]

For \(m=1\), \(q=a-1/2\) and \(c_1=0\), so \(\rho_1(1/g)=1/g-q^2g\), which for
\(q\ge0\) is positive exactly when \(qg<1\).  At \(a=1/2\) one has \(q=0\) and
the conclusion is immediate; for \(a>1/2\), \(qg<1\) is
\eqref{eq:trig-upper-half} at \(y=a\).
\end{proof}


The constant matrix is
\[
 M_0=
 \begin{pmatrix}g&\sqrt g\\ \sqrt g&1\end{pmatrix}
 =\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt g\\1\end{pmatrix}
 \begin{pmatrix}\sqrt g&1\end{pmatrix},
\]
a rank-one positive-semidefinite matrix.  With \(\psi_1(a+1)=g-a^{-2}\) and \(\beta_1=(2a-1)/(2a)\), the remaining coefficient matrix \(M_1\) has determinant
\begin{equation}\label{eq:det-M1}
 \det M_1
 =\psi_1(a+1)-g\beta_1^2
 =\frac{(4a-1)g-4}{4a^2}.
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:M1-indefinite}
There is \(a_*=0.3690738484\ldots\in(0,1/2)\) such that \(M_1\) is indefinite for
\(0<a<a_*\), positive semidefinite of rank one at \(a=a_*\), and positive definite
for \(a>a_*\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Set \(f(a)=(4a-1)\psi_1(a)-4\), so that \(\det M_1\) has the sign of \(f(a)\) by \eqref{eq:det-M1}.  Since \(g=\psi_1(a)=\sum_{r\ge0}(a+r)^{-2}\), for \(0<a<1/2\)
\[
 f'(a)=2\sum_{r\ge0}\frac{2r+1-2a}{(a+r)^3}>0,
\]
while \(f(a)\to-\infty\) as \(a\downarrow0\) and \(f(1/2)=\psi_1(1/2)-4=\pi^2/2-4>0\).  Hence \(f\) has a unique zero \(a_*\in(0,1/2)\).  The trichotomy follows with \Cref{thm:gram} for \(a\ge1/2\) and the positive diagonal of \(M_1\).
\end{proof}

Among the coefficient matrices only \(M_1\) can be indefinite at the endpoint
\((\kappa,\tau)=(1,1)\), so the determinant positivity of \Cref{thm:coefficientwise} cannot
follow from pointwise semidefiniteness of the \(M_m\) alone; see
\Cref{fig:defect-localization}.



\subsection{Finite-defect localization}\label{subsec:finite-defect}

The four-copy formula \eqref{eq:Delta-n-MD} makes the endpoint defect local in
total degree.  At \((\kappa,\tau)=(1,1)\), the only coefficient fiber that can
fail to be positive semidefinite is \(M_1\).  Hence the bad self-pair
\((M_1,M_1)\) can occur only when the two pair totals sum to \(2\).  Every
other determinant degree sees \(M_1\) only through mixed pairings with a
positive-definite fiber.  More generally, a unique potentially indefinite
fiber \(M_j\) in a \(2\times2\) matrix series can self-interact under
determinant polarization only in total degree \(2j\).

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:Delta-n-noneq2}
For every \(a>0\) and every \(n\ge1\) with \(n\ne2\), one has \(\Delta_n(a)>0\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The linear coefficient is strictly positive: \eqref{eq:trig-lower} gives
\(\psi_1(a)>1/a\), so \(ag>1\) and \(\MD(M_0,M_1)=(ag-1)/a^2>0\),
whence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Delta1-sharp}
 \Delta_1=S_0S_1\MD(M_0,M_1)
 =\frac{2(ag-1)}{a^3\Gamma(a)^4}>0.
\end{equation}
For \(m\ge2\) the mixed term satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:M1-Mm-positive}
 \MD(M_1,M_m)
 =\alpha_1(1+gc_m)+\alpha_m-2g\beta_1\beta_m>0.
\end{equation}
If \(a\ge1/2\), then \(M_1,M_m\succ0\) by \Cref{thm:gram} and the strict case of
\Cref{lem:MD-positive} applies; if \(0<a<1/2\), then \(\beta_1<0<\beta_m\) by
\eqref{eq:beta}, and since every diagonal entry is positive the right-hand side
is strictly positive.
For \(n\ge3\) the self-pair \((1,1)\) does not occur.  Classify the pairs \((k,n-k)\)
in \eqref{eq:Delta-n-MD}: if neither index is \(1\), both matrices in the pair are
positive semidefinite---\(M_0\) is rank-one positive semidefinite and \(M_m\succ0\)
for \(m\ge2\) by \Cref{thm:gram}---so the nonstrict case of \Cref{lem:MD-positive}
gives a nonnegative summand; if one index is \(1\), the other is at least \(2\) and
\eqref{eq:M1-Mm-positive} applies.  Every summand is therefore nonnegative, and
the pair \((M_0,M_n)\) is strictly positive because \(M_0\ne0\) and \(M_n\succ0\).
Hence \(\Delta_n>0\).
\end{proof}

\subsection{The exceptional degree and endpoint sufficiency}\label{subsec:endpoint-sufficiency}

The self-pairing \(\MD(M_1,M_1)=2\det M_1\) occurs solely in \(\Delta_2\), so the
indefiniteness established in \Cref{lem:M1-indefinite} can contribute negatively
only there.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:Delta2-positive}
For every \(a>0\), one has \(\Delta_2(a)>0\).  More precisely, let
\[
 t=\psi_1(a+1),
 \qquad
 \mathcal R_a=t-\frac1{a+1}.
\]
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Delta2-Q}
 \Delta_2=
 \frac{Q_*(a,t)}{2a^6(a+1)^3\Gamma(a)^4},
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align}
 Q_*(a,t)={}&2a^4(a+1)^2\mathcal R_a^2\notag\\
 &+2a^2(a+1)^2(8a^2+3a+1)\mathcal R_a\notag\\
 &+2a(a+1)(5a+3).\label{eq:Qstar-decomp}
\end{align}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
From \eqref{eq:Delta-n-MD},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Delta2-unsimplified}
 \Delta_2=S_0S_2\MD(M_0,M_2)+S_1^2\det M_1.
\end{equation}
Here
\[
 S_0=\frac1{\Gamma(a)^2},\qquad
 S_1=\frac2{a\Gamma(a)^2},\qquad
 S_2=\frac{2a+1}{a^2(a+1)\Gamma(a)^2},
\]
and, recalling
\(\det M_1=t-g\beta_1^2\) from \eqref{eq:det-M1},
\[
 \MD(M_0,M_2)
 =g+\frac{g^2}{2a+1}+\psi_1(a+2)
   -2g\frac{a}{a+1}.
\]
Substituting \(g=t+a^{-2}\), \(\psi_1(a+2)=t-(a+1)^{-2}\), and then
\(t=\mathcal R_a+(a+1)^{-1}\) into \eqref{eq:Delta2-unsimplified}, direct
collection over the denominator in \eqref{eq:Delta2-Q} gives the decomposition
\eqref{eq:Qstar-decomp}.

Applying \eqref{eq:trig-lower} at \(y=a+1\) gives
\(\mathcal R_a=\psi_1(a+1)-1/(a+1)>1/(2(a+1)^2)>0\).
All three coefficients in \eqref{eq:Qstar-decomp} are positive for \(a>0\),
so \(\Delta_2>0\).
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \Cref{thm:coefficientwise}, sufficiency]
For every \(a>0\) and \(n\ge1\), the endpoint assertion \(\Delta_n^{(1)}(a)>0\) holds
by \Cref{lem:Delta-n-noneq2} when \(n\ne2\) and by \Cref{lem:Delta2-positive} when
\(n=2\).  Since \(A\) and \(Z\) have positive coefficients and
\[
 [\lambda^n]Z_{\Thetal}=\frac{n}{n!\Gamma(a+n)}>0\qquad(n\ge1),
\]
every positive-degree coefficient of \(AZZ_{\Thetal}\) is strictly positive: in
degree \(n\), for example, the product of the constant coefficients of \(A\) and
\(Z\) with \([\lambda^n]Z_{\Thetal}\) is positive.  Therefore
\[
 \Delta^{(\kappa)}
 =\Delta^{(1)}+(\kappa-1)gA\,ZZ_{\Thetal}
\]
shows that all coefficients remain strictly positive for \(\kappa>1\).

Fix \(\kappa\ge1\).  Since \(\Delta^{(\kappa)}(a,\cdot)\) is entire, each differentiated Maclaurin series converges locally uniformly, so for every \(r\ge0\) and \(\lambda>0\),
\[
 \partial_\lambda^r\Delta^{(\kappa)}(a,\lambda)
 =\sum_{n\ge\max\{1,r\}}
 \frac{n!}{(n-r)!}\Delta_n^{(\kappa)}(a)\lambda^{n-r}>0,
\]
which makes \(\Delta^{(\kappa)}(a,\cdot)\) strictly absolutely monotone.  The leading principal entry satisfies \(A>0\) for \(\lambda>0\) by \eqref{eq:ABC-expansions} and \eqref{eq:alpha}; since \(\Delta^{(\kappa)}>0\), it follows that \(\Turan_\kappa\succ0\).
\end{proof}

The converse at each fixed \(a>0\) is deferred to the necessity part of
\Cref{thm:two-parameter-coeff}.

\begin{remark}\label{rem:uniform-degree-one}
Degree one already rules out any \(\kappa<1\) that is to serve every \(a>0\) at
once.  The general \(m=1\) coefficient on the slice \(\tau=1\) is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:MD01-kappa}
 \MD(M_0,M_1^{(\kappa,1)})
 =\frac{\frac{\kappa-1}{2}(ag)^2+ag-1}{a^2},
\end{equation}
and if \(\kappa<1\), then \(ag\to+\infty\) as \(a\downarrow0\), so
\eqref{eq:MD01-kappa}, and hence \(\Delta_1^{(\kappa)}\), is negative for all
sufficiently small \(a>0\).  This is weaker than the fixed-\(a\) necessity in
\Cref{thm:two-parameter-coeff}, which rules out every \(\kappa<1\) at each fixed \(a\).
\end{remark}

Combining \eqref{eq:Delta1-sharp} with \eqref{eq:trig-lower}, which gives
\(ag-1>(2a)^{-1}\), yields the explicit lower bound
\[
 \Delta(a,\lambda)>\Delta_1(a)\lambda
 =\frac{2\bigl(a\psi_1(a)-1\bigr)}{a^3\Gamma(a)^4}\,\lambda
 >\frac{\lambda}{a^4\Gamma(a)^4}
 \qquad(a,\lambda>0).
\]

\begin{proof}[Proof of \Cref{thm:bessel} and \Cref{cor:bessel-matrix}]
The congruence \eqref{eq:bessel-congruence} and the endpoint coefficient
positivity established in \Cref{subsec:endpoint-sufficiency} give positive
definiteness for \(z>0\), and
\eqref{eq:D-Delta} gives the determinant inequality.  As \(z\downarrow0\),
equivalently \(\lambda\downarrow0\), the power series for \(L=\log Z\) gives
\(L_{\Thetal},L_{a\Thetal},L_{\Thetal\Thetal}\to0\), while
\(Z(a,0)=1/\Gamma(a)\) gives \(L_{aa}\to-\psi_1(a)\).  Hence
\eqref{eq:U-L}--\eqref{eq:G-L} imply
\[
 G_\nu\longrightarrow g,\qquad
 1+P_\nu\longrightarrow2,\qquad
 H_\nu+\frac4g\longrightarrow\frac4g,
\]
which is the matrix limit in \Cref{cor:bessel-matrix}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Large argument and the deformation wall}\label{subsec:large-argument-deformation-wall}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:large-argument-limit}
For fixed \(\nu>-1\) and fixed \(\kappa,\tau\in\R\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:D-large-z-refined}
 D_\nu^{(\kappa,\tau)}(z)
 =2(\kappa-1)
 +\frac{4\tau/g-2(\kappa+1)\nu-\frac12}{z}
 +O(z^{-2})
 \qquad(z\to\infty).
\end{equation}
The remainder is uniform when \((\kappa,\tau)\) ranges over a fixed compact
subset of \(\R^2\).  In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:D-endpoint-tail}
 D_\nu(z)=\frac{c(a)}z+O(z^{-2}),
 \qquad a=\nu+1,
\end{equation}
and \(c(a)>3/2\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Differentiating a large-argument expansion of \(\log I_\nu\) requires a
zero-free complex neighborhood.  Fix \(\varphi\in(0,\pi/2)\), put
\(\Sigma_\varphi=\{w:|\arg w|\le\pi/2-\varphi\}\), and let \(K\subset\mathbb C\)
be a closed disc about the given order.  Uniformly for \(\mu\in K\) and
\(w\in\Sigma_\varphi\), the Hankel expansion and its error bounds in
\cite[eqs.~10.17.1 and 10.40.1, \S10.40(iii)]{NISTDLMFBessel} give
\[
 (2\pi w)^{1/2}e^{-w}I_\mu(w)
 =1-\frac{4\mu^2-1}{8w}
 +\frac{(4\mu^2-1)(4\mu^2-9)}{128w^2}
 +O(w^{-3}).
\]
For sufficiently large \(|w|\), the left side lies in a fixed disc about \(1\),
so its principal logarithm is holomorphic.  Taking logarithms gives, uniformly
on a slightly smaller product domain,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:log-I-two-term}
 \log I_\mu(w)
 =w-\frac12\log(2\pi w)
 -\frac{4\mu^2-1}{8w}
 -\frac{4\mu^2-1}{16w^2}
 +O(w^{-3}).
\end{equation}
The remainder is holomorphic in \((\mu,w)\) on that domain, and for
\(\varepsilon=\varepsilon(\varphi)\) small the closed disc of radius
\(\varepsilon|w|\) about \(w\) lies in \(\Sigma_\varphi\); Cauchy
estimates over that disc and over a fixed circle in the order variable
therefore justify differentiating the remainder twice in \(\mu\) and twice in
\(w\), each differentiation in \(w\) costing one power of \(|w|\).

At \(w=z>0\), differentiation of \eqref{eq:log-I-two-term} yields
\[
 G_\nu=\frac1z+\frac1{2z^2}+O(z^{-3}),\qquad
 P_\nu=\frac\nu z+\frac\nu{z^2}+O(z^{-3}),
\]
and
\[
 H_\nu^{(\kappa)}
 =(2\kappa-1)z-\kappa(1+2\nu)
 +\frac{(2\kappa+1)(4\nu^2-1)}{8z}
 +O(z^{-2}).
\]
Substitution into \eqref{eq:Dnu-kt-def} gives
\eqref{eq:D-large-z-refined}.  Since \(\kappa\) and \(\tau\) enter these
expressions only affinely, the remainder is uniform on compact subsets of the
\((\kappa,\tau)\)-plane.  At \((\kappa,\tau)=(1,1)\), its \(z^{-1}\)
coefficient is \(c(a)\).  Finally \eqref{eq:inverse-trig} gives
\[
 c(a)=\frac4g-4a+\frac72>4\left(a-\frac12\right)-4a+\frac72=\frac32.
\]
On the other hand, \eqref{eq:trig-lower} gives \(g>1/a\), hence \(1/g<a\),
so \(c(a)<7/2\).  This proves \eqref{eq:c-range}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The coefficient walls and the exposed degree-one boundary}\label{subsec:coefficient-walls-exposed-degree}

We need one endpoint lemma for the lower boundary.  Put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tau-star-s-star}
 \tau_*=\tau_{\mathrm{cw}}(a,1)=\frac{ag(2a-1)}{2a^2g-1},
 \qquad
 s_*=\frac{\tau_*}{g}=\frac{a(2a-1)}{2a^2g-1}.
\end{equation}
For the coefficient matrices on this boundary, normalize by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Nhat-m}
 \widehat N_m
 =\diag(1,g^{-1/2})M_m^{(1,\tau_*)}\diag(1,g^{-1/2})
 =\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_m&\beta_m\\ \beta_m&s_*+c_m\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:boundary-positivity}
Let \(a>0\) and let
\(\widehat\Delta_n=[\lambda^n]\Delta^{(1,\tau_*)}(a,\lambda)\).  Then
\[
 \widehat\Delta_1=0,
 \qquad
 \widehat\Delta_n>0\quad(n\ge2).
\]
\end{lemma}

The proof is a four-part verification: positivity of the fibers with
\(m\ge2\), positivity of mixed pairs involving the vacuum fiber, positivity of
mixed pairs involving the one-particle fiber, and a separate check of the
degree-two self-pair.  The required
trigamma estimates and algebraic reductions are collected in
\Cref{app:boundary-proof}.


We now write the coefficientwise region as the upper envelope of its
individual degree walls.  Put \(p_n=[\lambda^n]P\) and \(q_n=[\lambda^n]Q\).
Since \(ZZ_{\Thetal}=\frac12\Thetal(Z^2)\), \Cref{thm:coefficients} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pq-coefficients}
 p_n=\sum_{k=0}^nS_kS_{n-k}\alpha_k,
 \qquad
 q_n=\frac g2\sum_{k=0}^n(n-k)S_kS_{n-k}\alpha_k.
\end{equation}
Recall the individual degree wall \(\tau_n(a,\kappa)\) from
\eqref{eq:coefficient-wall}.  The affine determinant identity is exactly
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coefficient-wall-factor}
 [\lambda^n]\Delta^{(\kappa,\tau)}
 =p_n\bigl(\tau-\tau_n(a,\kappa)\bigr).
\end{equation}
Thus positive-degree coefficientwise nonnegativity is the intersection of the
half-planes \(\tau\ge\tau_n(a,\kappa)\).

Under the symmetric law \eqref{eq:Tn-Kn-law}, \(\E K_n=n/2\), and
\eqref{eq:pq-coefficients} gives the exact slope identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wall-slope-covariance}
 \frac{q_n}{p_n}
 =\frac{gn}{4}
 -\frac g2\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(K_n,\alpha_{K_n})}
 {\E\alpha_{K_n}}.
\end{equation}
Since \(k\mapsto\alpha_k=\psi_1(a+k)\) is strictly decreasing and the law of
\(K_n\) is nondegenerate for \(n\ge1\),
\(\operatorname{Cov}(K_n,\alpha_{K_n})<0\).  Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qn-pn-ratio}
 \frac{q_n}{p_n}>\frac{gn}{4}\qquad(n\ge1).
\end{equation}
For \(n=1\), direct calculation gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:q1-p1}
 \frac{q_1}{p_1}
 =\frac{a^2g^2}{2(2a^2g-1)}<\frac g2,
\end{equation}
because \(a^2g>1\).  For \(n\ge2\), \eqref{eq:qn-pn-ratio} gives
\(q_n/p_n>gn/4\ge g/2>q_1/p_1\), hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:q-ratio-gap}
 \frac{q_n}{p_n}>\frac{q_1}{p_1}\qquad(n\ge2).
\end{equation}
Equivalently, the higher-degree walls have strictly more negative
\(\kappa\)-slope than the degree-one wall.

\begin{proof}[Proof of \Cref{thm:two-parameter-coeff}]
At \(\kappa=1\), \Cref{lem:boundary-positivity} says precisely that
\[
 \tau_1(a,1)=\tau_*,
 \qquad
 \tau_n(a,1)<\tau_1(a,1)\quad(n\ge2).
\]
Since \(\partial_\kappa\tau_n=-q_n/p_n\), \eqref{eq:q-ratio-gap} gives, for
\(\kappa\ge1\) and \(n\ge2\),
\[
 \tau_1(a,\kappa)-\tau_n(a,\kappa)
 =\tau_1(a,1)-\tau_n(a,1)
 +(\kappa-1)\left(\frac{q_n}{p_n}-\frac{q_1}{p_1}\right)>0.
\]
Thus degree one is the unique exposed positive-degree wall and
\[
 \sup_{n\ge1}\tau_n(a,\kappa)
 =\tau_1(a,\kappa)
 =\tau_*-(\kappa-1)\frac{q_1}{p_1}
 =\tau_{\mathrm{cw}}(a,\kappa).
\]
Equation \eqref{eq:coefficient-wall-factor} now gives the strict and nonstrict
coefficientwise assertions, including positivity of every degree \(n\ge2\) on
the boundary.

It remains to exclude \(\kappa<1\).  Fix \(\tau\).  By
\Cref{lem:large-argument-limit},
\(D_\nu^{(\kappa,\tau)}(z)\to2(\kappa-1)<0\).  Since
\(Z(a,\lambda)^4\to\infty\), \eqref{eq:Dkappa-tau-Delta} forces
\(\Delta^{(\kappa,\tau)}(a,\lambda)\to-\infty\).  A power series whose
positive-degree coefficients are all nonnegative is bounded below on
\((0,\infty)\) by its constant coefficient, so some positive-degree
coefficient must be negative.

Finally,
\[
 \tau_*(a)<1
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 a\psi_1(a)>1,
\]
which follows from \eqref{eq:trig-lower}; and
\(\tau_{\mathrm{cw}}(a,\kappa)\) decreases with \(\kappa\).  This proves the
last assertion as well.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \Cref{prop:bessel-sharpness}]
The endpoint theorem gives \(D_\nu>0\).  From \eqref{eq:Dnu-kt-def},
\[
 D_\nu^{(\kappa,\tau)}
 =D_\nu
 +2(\kappa-1)G_\nu(\Thetaz\log I_\nu-\nu)
 +\frac{4(\tau-1)}gG_\nu.
\]
For \(\nu>-1,z>0\), both \(G_\nu=A/Z^2\) and
\(\Thetaz\log I_\nu-\nu=2L_{\Thetal}\) are strictly positive.  Thus
\(\kappa\ge1,\tau\ge1\) is sufficient.

As \(z\downarrow0\), the power series gives
\(D_\nu^{(\kappa,\tau)}(z)\to4(\tau-1)\),
so pointwise positivity forces \(\tau\ge1\).  As \(z\to\infty\),
\Cref{lem:large-argument-limit} gives the limit \(2(\kappa-1)\), so it also
forces \(\kappa\ge1\).  This proves the sharp quadrant.

Now suppose
\(\kappa\ge1\) and
\(\tau_{\mathrm{cw}}(a,\kappa)\le\tau<1\).  By
\Cref{thm:two-parameter-coeff}, every positive-degree coefficient of
\(\Delta^{(\kappa,\tau)}\) is nonnegative and every coefficient of degree at
least two is positive, while \eqref{eq:Delta0-tau} is negative.  Therefore
\[
 \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\lambda}\Delta^{(\kappa,\tau)}(a,\lambda)>0
 \qquad(\lambda>0),
\]
and \(\Delta^{(\kappa,\tau)}(a,\lambda)\to+\infty\) as
\(\lambda\to\infty\).  It has exactly one positive zero.  The positive factor
in \eqref{eq:Dkappa-tau-Delta} transfers the same zero to
\(D_\nu^{(\kappa,\tau)}\).
\end{proof}

Taking \(\tau=1\) in the necessity part of
\Cref{thm:two-parameter-coeff} supplies the deferred converse in
\Cref{thm:coefficientwise}; together with the endpoint sufficiency proved in
\Cref{subsec:endpoint-sufficiency}, this completes that theorem.


\section{Critical wall fan and equivalence of ensembles}\label{sec:scaling}

The high-degree wall fan focuses at its thermodynamic accumulation point.  There
the coefficient--argument correspondence becomes an instance of equivalence
between the canonical and microcanonical ensembles.

\subsection{The central microcanonical law}\label{subsec:central-microcanonical-law}

With the four-copy conditional variable \(K_n\) from \eqref{eq:Tn-Kn-law}, put
\(X_n=(K_n-n/2)/\sqrt n\).
Thus \(X_n\) is the centered first-pair total under the microcanonical
constraint \(Y_1+Y_2+Y_3+Y_4=n\).
By \eqref{eq:Delta-n-MD} and \eqref{eq:pq-coefficients}, symmetry gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:threshold-expectation}
 n(1-\kappa_n)
 =\frac{n\,\E\MD(M_{K_n},M_{n-K_n})}
 {g\,\E[(n-K_n)\alpha_{K_n}]}.
\end{equation}

The law of \(K_n\) is a slowly varying tilt of a symmetric hypergeometric law.
We record a quantitative form because it is what makes the remainder in
\Cref{thm:critical-scaling} effective.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:central-moments}
For fixed \(a>0\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:X2-asymptotic}
 \E X_n^2
 =\frac18+\frac{2a-1}{8n}+O_a(n^{-2}),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:X4-asymptotic}
 \E X_n^4=\frac3{64}+O_a(n^{-1}),
 \qquad
 \E|X_n|^6=O_a(1).
\end{equation}
More strongly, for every fixed \(q,M\ge0\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tilted-tail}
 \E\!\left[(1+|X_n|^q)
 \mathbf 1_{\{|K_n-n/2|>n^{3/5}\}}\right]
 =O_{a,q,M}(n^{-M}).
\end{equation}
All these estimates are uniform when \(a\) ranges over a compact subinterval
of \((0,\infty)\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The duplication formula rewrites \(S_m\), for all sufficiently large \(m\), as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Sm-gamma-ratio}
 S_m=\frac{2^{2a-2}}{\sqrt\pi}\frac{4^m}{(m!)^2}
 \frac{\Gamma(m+a-1/2)}{\Gamma(m+a)}
 \frac{\Gamma(m+1)}{\Gamma(m+2a-1)}.
\end{equation}
The standard gamma-ratio expansion \cite[\S5.11(iii)]{NISTDLMFGamma} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Sm-asymptotic}
 S_m=C_a\frac{4^m}{(m!)^2}m^b
 \left(1+\frac{s_a}{m}+O_a(m^{-2})\right),
\end{equation}
where
\[
 C_a=\frac{2^{2a-2}}{\sqrt\pi},\qquad
 b=\frac32-2a,\qquad
 s_a=-2a^2+\frac52a-\frac58.
\]
If the slowly varying factors are omitted, \eqref{eq:Tn-Kn-law} becomes
\[
 \pi_{n,k}=\frac{\binom nk^2}{\binom{2n}n},
\]
the hypergeometric law obtained by drawing \(n\) points without replacement
from a population of size \(2n\) containing \(n\) marked points.

For quantitative tails, set
\[
 h_m(a)=\frac{(m!)^2}{4^m}S_m(a),
 \qquad
 u_m(a)=\frac{h_m(a)}{(m+1)^b}.
\]
By \eqref{eq:Sm-asymptotic}, \(u_m(a)\to C_a>0\).  Hence, for fixed \(a\),
\[
 0<m_a:=\inf_{m\ge0}u_m(a)
 \le\sup_{m\ge0}u_m(a)=:M_a<\infty.
\]
The exact law of \(K_n\) is
\[
 \mathbb P(K_n=k)
 =\frac{\pi_{n,k}h_kh_{n-k}}
 {\E_{\pi_n}[h_Kh_{n-K}]}.
\]
On the event \(|K-n/2|\le\sqrt n\), whose \(\pi_n\)-probability is bounded
away from zero by the exact variance below and Chebyshev's inequality, the
factor \(h_Kh_{n-K}\) is bounded below by a positive multiple of \(n^{2b}\),
because \(k+1\) and \(n-k+1\) are both of order \(n\) there.  Globally
\(h_kh_{n-k}\le M_a^2[(k+1)(n-k+1)]^b\), and on \(0\le k\le n\) the product
\((k+1)(n-k+1)\) lies in \([n+1,(n/2+1)^2]\), so that bound is largest near the
center when \(b\ge0\) and at the endpoints when \(b<0\); in both cases it is a
positive multiple of \(n^{2b+(-b)_+}\).  Therefore there is a finite constant
\(C_{\mathrm{tilt}}(a)\) such that, for every event \(A\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tilt-comparison}
 \mathbb P(K_n\in A)
 \le C_{\mathrm{tilt}}(a)n^{(-b)_+}\pi_n(A).
\end{equation}
The constants may be chosen uniformly for \(a\) in a compact subinterval of
\((0,\infty)\).

Hoeffding's sampling-without-replacement inequality
\cite[\S6]{Hoeffding1963Probability} gives
\[
 \pi_n\{|K-n/2|>t\}\le2e^{-2t^2/n}.
\]
With \(t=n^{3/5}\), \eqref{eq:tilt-comparison} yields
\[
 \mathbb P\{|K_n-n/2|>n^{3/5}\}
 \le2C_{\mathrm{tilt}}(a)n^{(-b)_+}e^{-2n^{1/5}},
\]
which implies \eqref{eq:tilted-tail} because \(|X_n|\le\sqrt n/2\).

Under the base law \(\pi_n\), for \(X=(K-n/2)/\sqrt n\), exact factorial
moments give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hypergeom-moments}
 \E_0X^2=\frac{n}{4(2n-1)}
 =\frac18+\frac1{16n}+O(n^{-2}),
\end{equation}
\[
 \E_0X^4
 =\frac{n(3n-4)}{16(2n-3)(2n-1)}
 =\frac3{64}+O(n^{-1}),
 \qquad
 \E_0|X|^6=O(1).
\]
On the central window \(|K-n/2|\le n^{3/5}\), one has
\(|x|\le n^{1/10}=o(n^{1/6})\) and
\[
 [k(n-k)]^b
 =\left(\frac{n^2}{4}\right)^b
 \left(1-\frac{4bx^2}{n}
 +O_a\left(\frac{1+x^4}{n^2}\right)\right).
\]
The product of the \(s_a/m\) corrections in \eqref{eq:Sm-asymptotic} is
\(1+4s_a/n+O_a((1+x^2)/n^2)\), and its order-\(1/n\) constant cancels under
normalization.  The super-polynomial tail estimate permits termwise averaging,
so
\[
 \E X_n^2
 =\E_0X^2-\frac{4b}{n}
 \left(\E_0X^4-(\E_0X^2)^2\right)+O_a(n^{-2}).
\]
Substitution proves \eqref{eq:X2-asymptotic}; the fourth- and sixth-moment
claims follow in the same way.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Asymptotics of the degree thresholds}\label{subsec:asymptotics-degree-thresholds}

The exact formulas in \Cref{thm:coefficients} have the large-\(m\) expansions
\begin{align}
 \alpha_m&=\frac1m+\frac{1/2-a}{m^2}
 +\frac{a^2-a+1/6}{m^3}+O(m^{-4}),\label{eq:alpha-asymptotic}\\
 \beta_m&=\frac12+\frac{a-1}{2m}
 -\frac{(a-1)^2}{2m^2}+O(m^{-3}),\label{eq:beta-asymptotic}\\
 c_m&=\frac m4-\frac{a-1/2}{4}
 +\frac{(a-3/2)(a-1/2)}{4m}+O(m^{-2}).\label{eq:c-asymptotic}
\end{align}
For \(k=n/2+x\sqrt n\) and \(\ell=n-k\), substitution in the mixed determinant
gives, uniformly on any growing central window on which
\(|x|=o(n^{1/6})\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:MD-central-expansion}
 \MD(M_k,M_\ell)
 =\frac{\Phi_1(x)}n+\frac{\Phi_2(x)}{n^2}
 +O\left(\frac{1+|x|^6}{n^3}\right),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Phi1}
 \Phi_1(x)=4+g(3-4a)+4gx^2
\end{equation}
and
\begin{align}
 \Phi_2(x)=\frac13\bigl(&48gx^4+(60-96a)gx^2+48x^2\notag\\
 &+24a^2g-36ag-24a+13g+12\bigr).\label{eq:Phi2}
\end{align}
Likewise, symmetry gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:den-central-expansion}
 \frac12\bigl[\ell\alpha_k+k\alpha_\ell\bigr]
 =1+\frac{1-2a+8x^2}{n}
 +O\left(\frac{1+x^4}{n^2}\right).
\end{equation}
The tail estimates in \Cref{lem:central-moments} make these expansions
integrable under the law of \(K_n\).  The large-\(m\) expansions above and the
central-window remainders are uniform when \(a\) ranges over a compact
subinterval of \((0,\infty)\).  On the central window, the remainders in
\eqref{eq:MD-central-expansion} and \eqref{eq:den-central-expansion} have
expectations \(O_a(n^{-3})\) and \(O_a(n^{-2})\), respectively, by
\eqref{eq:X4-asymptotic}.  Off that window, uniformly for \(a\) in such compact
sets, the exact coefficient formulas give \(\MD(M_k,M_{n-k})=O(n)\) and
\((n-k)\alpha_k=O(n)\), with uniform implied constants; choosing \(M\)
sufficiently large in \eqref{eq:tilted-tail} makes those tail contributions
smaller than the displayed remainders.

Taking expectations and using \eqref{eq:X2-asymptotic}--\eqref{eq:X4-asymptotic}
gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:MD-expectation-two-term}
 \E\MD(M_{K_n},M_{n-K_n})
 =\frac{gc(a)}n
 +\frac{96a^2g-180ag-96a+85g+72}{12n^2}
 +O_a(n^{-3}),
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:den-expectation}
 \E[(n-K_n)\alpha_{K_n}]
 =1+\frac{2(1-a)}n+O_a(n^{-2}).
\end{equation}
More explicitly,
\[
 \E\Phi_1(X_n)
 =gc(a)+\frac{g(2a-1)}{2n}+O_a(n^{-2}),
\]
while
\[
 \E\Phi_2(X_n)
 =\frac{96a^2g-192ag-96a+91g+72}{12}+O_a(n^{-1}).
\]
Combining these two contributions gives the second coefficient in
\eqref{eq:MD-expectation-two-term}.  At leading order the \(4gx^2\) term in
\eqref{eq:Phi1} contributes \(4g\cdot(1/8)=g/2\); this Gaussian fluctuation is
the source of the additional \(1/2\) in the critical constant.

\begin{proof}[Proof of \Cref{thm:critical-scaling}]
Insert \eqref{eq:MD-expectation-two-term} and \eqref{eq:den-expectation} into
\eqref{eq:threshold-expectation}.  Division gives
\[
 n(1-\kappa_n)
 =c(a)+\frac{g-24}{12g}\frac1n+O_a(n^{-2}),
\]
which is equivalent to \eqref{eq:kappa-n-two-term}.  The first equality in
\eqref{eq:critical-constant-match} follows from \eqref{eq:D-endpoint-tail}, and
the second from the leading term just proved.  The bounds in \eqref{eq:c-range}
were proved in \Cref{lem:large-argument-limit}; strict monotonicity and the
endpoint limits of \(c(a)\) are proved in \Cref{prop:c-monotone} below.

Since \(c(a)>3/2\),
\[
 \kappa_{n+1}-\kappa_n
 =\frac{c(a)}{n^2}+O_a(n^{-3})>0
\]
for all sufficiently large \(n\).  Finally,
\(\Delta_n^{(\kappa)}=q_n(\kappa-\kappa_n)\) with \(q_n>0\).  Since the endpoint
coefficients satisfy \(\Delta_n>0\), one has \(\kappa_n<1\) for every \(n\).
Thus for each fixed \(J\), \(\max_{1\le n\le J}\kappa_n<1\), so
\(N_a(\kappa)>J\) once \(\kappa\) is sufficiently close to \(1\); in particular,
\(N_a(\kappa)\to\infty\) as \(\kappa\uparrow1\).  The first negative coefficient
therefore lies in the eventually monotone tail.  Since
\(1-\kappa_n=c(a)/n+O_a(n^{-2})\), it is the first index for which
\(1-\kappa_n<1-\kappa\), and inversion gives
\eqref{eq:first-negative-asymptotic}.
The two-term expansion in fact yields
\(N_a(\kappa)=c(a)/(1-\kappa)+O(1)\).
\end{proof}

The critical-scaling theorem strengthens the fixed-\(a\) necessity in
\Cref{thm:two-parameter-coeff}: below the deformation wall, negativity is not merely
present in some degree but eventually persists throughout the coefficient tail.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:eventual-negative-tail}
Fix \(a>0\) and \(\kappa<1\).  Then \(\Delta_n^{(\kappa)}(a)<0\)
for every sufficiently large \(n\).
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By \eqref{eq:kappa-n-two-term}, \(\kappa_n(a)\to1\).  Since
\(\Delta_n^{(\kappa)}=q_n(\kappa-\kappa_n)\) and \(q_n>0\), one has
\(\kappa<\kappa_n\) for all sufficiently large \(n\).
\end{proof}


\subsection{The coefficient-wall fan}\label{subsec:wall-fan}

The same central law gives the geometry of every fixed-\(\tau\) deformation
threshold; \Cref{fig:wall-fan} shows the resulting fan at \(a=1\).  From
\eqref{eq:pq-coefficients} and \eqref{eq:Tn-Kn-law},
\[
 \frac{p_n}{T_n}=\E\alpha_{K_n},
 \qquad
 \frac{q_n}{T_n}=\frac g2\E[(n-K_n)\alpha_{K_n}].
\]
Using \eqref{eq:alpha-asymptotic}, symmetry, \eqref{eq:X2-asymptotic}, and the
tail estimate \eqref{eq:tilted-tail}, termwise averaging gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:p-over-T-two-term}
 \frac{p_n}{T_n}
 =\frac2n+\frac{3-4a}{n^2}+O_a(n^{-3}).
\end{equation}
Together with \eqref{eq:den-expectation}, this yields the sharper ratio
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pq-ratio-two-term}
 \frac{p_n}{q_n}
 =\frac4{gn}-\frac2{gn^2}+O_a(n^{-3}).
\end{equation}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \Cref{thm:wall-fan}]
By \eqref{eq:kappa-n-tau-def},
\[
 1-\kappa_{n,\tau}
 =(1-\kappa_n)+(\tau-1)\frac{p_n}{q_n}.
\]
Substitution of \eqref{eq:kappa-n-two-term} and
\eqref{eq:pq-ratio-two-term} gives
\eqref{eq:vertical-wall-two-term} with \(c_\tau,d_\tau\) as in
\eqref{eq:c-d-tau}.  The compact-uniform versions of the same estimates give
local uniformity in \((a,\tau)\).

For the Bessel side, extend \eqref{eq:log-I-two-term} by one term.  Uniformly
for the order in a compact set and \(z\) in a fixed proper right-half-plane
sector,
\begin{align}\label{eq:log-I-three-term}
 \log I_\nu(z)={}&z-\frac12\log(2\pi z)
 -\frac{4\nu^2-1}{8z}
 -\frac{4\nu^2-1}{16z^2}\notag\\
 &+\frac{(4\nu^2-1)(4\nu^2-25)}{384z^3}
 +O(z^{-4}).
\end{align}
The same Cauchy-estimate argument used in \Cref{lem:large-argument-limit}
permits the required order and argument derivatives.  Substitution in
\eqref{eq:Dnu-kt-def} at \(\kappa=1\) gives
\[
 D_{a-1}^{(1,\tau)}(z)
 =\frac{4\tau/g-4a+7/2}{z}
 +\frac{2\tau/g-3(a-1)-1/3}{z^2}
 +O_{a,\tau}(z^{-3}),
\]
which is \eqref{eq:D-wall-two-term}.

The first coefficient \(c_\tau\) vanishes precisely at
\(\tau=\tau_\infty(a)=g(a-7/8)\).  At this value,
\[
 d_{\tau_\infty}(a)=2\left(\frac{11}{12}-a\right),
 \qquad
 e_{\tau_\infty}(a)=\frac{11}{12}-a,
\]
which proves \eqref{eq:second-order-match}.

The coefficient walls of \eqref{eq:coefficient-wall} satisfy, at \(\kappa=1\),
\[
 \tau_n(a,1)=1-\frac{\Delta_n}{p_n}.
\]
Since \(\Delta_n/q_n=1-\kappa_n\),
\eqref{eq:kappa-n-two-term} and \eqref{eq:pq-ratio-two-term} give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tau-n-limit}
 \lim_{n\to\infty}\tau_n(a,1)
 =1-\frac{gc(a)}4
 =g\left(a-\frac78\right)
 =\tau_\infty(a).
\end{equation}
Thus \(\tau_\infty\) is the thermodynamic accumulation point of the
positive-degree wall fan.

The inequality \(\tau_{\mathrm{cw}}(a,1)<1\) was proved in
\Cref{thm:two-parameter-coeff}.  For the other side, direct subtraction gives
\[
 \tau_{\mathrm{cw}}(a,1)-\tau_\infty(a)
 =-\frac{g\{16a^3g-14a^2g-16a^2+7\}}
 {8(2a^2g-1)}.
\]
If \(a\le7/8\), the lower bound in \eqref{eq:trig-lower} makes the expression
in braces at most \(-6a<0\), with strict inequality for \(a<7/8\).  If \(a\ge7/8\), the upper bound
\eqref{eq:trig-upper-cubic} makes it strictly smaller than
\(-(18a^2-8a+7)/(3a)<0\).
Hence \(\tau_\infty<\tau_{\mathrm{cw}}(a,1)\), completing
\eqref{eq:wall-ordering}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:c-monotone}
The function \(c(a)=4/\psi_1(a)-4a+7/2\)
is strictly decreasing on \((0,\infty)\), with
\[
 \lim_{a\downarrow0}c(a)=\frac72,
 \qquad
 \lim_{a\to\infty}c(a)=\frac32.
\]
Thus \((3/2,7/2)\) is its exact range.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The inequality \(\psi_1(a)^2+\psi_2(a)>0\) used below is classical in the
polygamma-inequality literature; see Alzer \cite[eq.~(4.39) in the proof of Thm.~4.8]{Alzer2004Polygamma}.  We keep
the following short derivation to make the monotonicity argument
self-contained.  Write
\[
 g(a)=\psi_1(a)=\int_0^\infty e^{-at}k(t)\dd t,
 \qquad
 k(t)=\frac{t}{1-e^{-t}}.
\]
Then
\[
 g(a)^2=\int_0^\infty e^{-as}(k*k)(s)\dd s,
 \qquad
 -g'(a)=\int_0^\infty e^{-as}\frac{s^2}{1-e^{-s}}\dd s.
\]
For \(0<t<s\),
\[
 \frac{1-e^{-s}}
 {(1-e^{-t})(1-e^{-(s-t)})}
 =\frac1{1-e^{-t}}+\frac1{1-e^{-(s-t)}}-1.
\]
Using \(t/(1-e^{-t})=\frac t2\coth\frac t2+\frac t2>1+\frac t2\), valid because
\(x\coth x>1\) for \(x>0\) (indeed, \(x\cosh x-\sinh x\) vanishes at \(0\)
and has derivative \(x\sinh x>0\)), and symmetry,
\begin{align*}
 (1-e^{-s})(k*k)(s)
 &=2\int_0^s\frac{t(s-t)}{1-e^{-t}}\dd t
   -\int_0^s t(s-t)\dd t\\
 &>2\int_0^s(s-t)\left(1+\frac t2\right)\dd t-\frac{s^3}{6}
 =s^2.
\end{align*}
Hence \(g^2+g'>0\), and therefore
\[
 c'(a)=-4\frac{g^2+g'}{g^2}<0.
\]
As \(a\downarrow0\), the partial-fraction series gives
\(g(a)=a^{-2}+O(1)\), so \(c(a)\to7/2\).  As \(a\to\infty\),
\Cref{lem:trigamma-bounds} gives
\[
 g(a)=\frac1a+\frac1{2a^2}+O(a^{-3}),
\]
hence \(1/g=a-1/2+O(a^{-1})\) and \(c(a)\to3/2\).
\end{proof}

\subsection{Equivalence of ensembles and the coefficient--argument dictionary}\label{subsec:ensemble-equivalence}

The exact identity \eqref{eq:D-canonical-average} already places the pointwise
and coefficient problems in one ensemble.  Conditioning exponential-family
samples on sufficient statistics and comparing the conditioned laws with
tilted product laws has a classical general theory
\cite{DiaconisFreedman1988Conditional}; for the broader canonical--microcanonical
ensemble-equivalence terminology see, for example,
\cite{Touchette2015Ensembles}.  From the generating-function side, the use of
a parameterized random-size law and its conditioning or tuning to fixed size is
the classical Boltzmann paradigm \cite{Duchon2004Boltzmann}; recent work
also develops fixed-size/random-size transfer for moment statistics
\cite{Nebel2026BoltzmannMoments}.  The present argument is more explicit: the
finite Bessel sector decomposition is exact, its sectors carry the
Schur-deficit and mixed-determinant structure, and the remaining scale
dictionary is concentration of the four-copy canonical total.  From
\eqref{eq:bessel-law-meanvar} and \eqref{eq:U-L},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:N-mean}
 \E_\lambda N=4L_{\Thetal}
 =2(\Thetaz\log I_{a-1}(z)-(a-1))
 =2z-(2a-1)+O_a(z^{-1}).
\end{equation}
Moreover, \eqref{eq:H-dispersion} at \(\kappa=1\) gives
\[
 \operatorname{Var}N=4\operatorname{Var}Y<4\E Y=\E N=O_a(z).
\]
Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ensemble-equivalence}
 \frac{N}{2z}\longrightarrow1
 \qquad(z\to\infty)
\end{equation}
in probability.  In particular, the microcanonical total corresponding to the
canonical argument \(z\) is \(n\sim2z\).

The usual coefficient saddle is the analytic form of the same identity:
for \(Z^4\), the radial stationarity equation is
\[
 n=\lambda\partial_\lambda\log Z^4=\E_\lambda N.
\]
Thus the factor \(2\) in the coefficient--argument correspondence is not an
independent saddle coincidence; it is equivalence of the canonical and
microcanonical descriptions.

For example, when \(\tau=1\) and \(\kappa=1-\varepsilon\),
\Cref{lem:large-argument-limit} gives, uniformly for small \(\varepsilon\ge0\),
\[
 D_{a-1}^{(1-\varepsilon,1)}(z)
 =-2\varepsilon+\frac{c(a)+2\varepsilon(a-1)}z+O_a(z^{-2}).
\]
The sign-bracketing argument with \(z=s/\varepsilon\) gives a positive zero
\(z_\varepsilon\) satisfying \(z_\varepsilon\sim c(a)/(2\varepsilon)\).
Together with \eqref{eq:first-negative-asymptotic},
\(N_a(1-\varepsilon)\sim2z_\varepsilon\),
exactly as predicted by \eqref{eq:ensemble-equivalence}.

At fixed \((\kappa,\tau)\), the sector densities themselves satisfy
\[
 d_n^{(\kappa,\tau)}
 =\frac{4q_n}{gT_n}\bigl(\kappa-\kappa_{n,\tau}\bigr),
\]
so \eqref{eq:den-expectation} and \eqref{eq:vertical-wall-two-term} give
\[
 \lim_{n\to\infty}d_n^{(\kappa,\tau)}=2(\kappa-1).
\]
The three phase walls are therefore literally the contacts of the sector
family at \(n=0\), \(n=1\), and \(n=\infty\).

\subsection{The cubic multicritical point}\label{subsec:cubic-point}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \Cref{thm:cubic-multicritical}]
Recall \eqref{eq:cubic-point}.  At this value
the gamma-ratio expansion \eqref{eq:Sm-gamma-ratio} can be continued to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Sm-cubic-expansion}
 S_m=C_*\frac{4^m}{(m!)^2}m^{-1/3}
 \left(1-\frac1{72m}-\frac{169}{10368m^2}+O(m^{-3})\right),
 \qquad C_*=C_{11/12}>0.
\end{equation}
On the central scale \(k=n/2+x\sqrt n\), \(\ell=n-k\), the slowly varying
tilt relative to the symmetric hypergeometric law is therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cubic-tilt}
 1+\frac{24x^2-1}{18n}
 +\frac{192x^4-16x^2-7}{54n^2}
 +O\!\left(\frac{1+|x|^6}{n^3}\right),
\end{equation}
up to a factor independent of \(x\).  The base hypergeometric moments satisfy
\[
 \E_0X^2=\frac{n}{4(2n-1)},
 \qquad
 \E_0X^4=\frac{n(3n-4)}{16(2n-3)(2n-1)},
\]
\[
 \E_0X^6
 =\frac{15n^3-60n^2+64n-16}
 {64(2n-5)(2n-3)(2n-1)}.
\]
Normalizing \eqref{eq:cubic-tilt} and averaging these exact moments gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cubic-moments}
 \E X_n^2
 =\frac18+\frac{5}{48n}+\frac{35}{288n^2}+O(n^{-3}),
\end{equation}
\[
 \E X_n^4=\frac3{64}+\frac1{16n}+O(n^{-2}),
 \qquad
 \E X_n^6=\frac{15}{512}+O(n^{-1}),
 \qquad
 \E X_n^8=O(1).
\]
The tail estimate \eqref{eq:tilted-tail} remains available with arbitrary
polynomial weight.

Define
\[
 \mathcal H_{n,k}
 =\frac12\MD(M_k,M_\ell)
 +(\tau_{\mathrm{mc}}-1)\frac{\alpha_k+\alpha_\ell}{2}.
\]
Expanding the exact coefficient formulas one order beyond
\eqref{eq:MD-central-expansion} gives, uniformly on
\(|x|=o(n^{1/8})\),
\begin{align}\label{eq:cubic-integrand}
 \mathcal H_{n,k}={}&
 \frac{g_{\mathrm{mc}}(8x^2-1)}{4n}
 +\frac{g_{\mathrm{mc}}(192x^4-104x^2-1)}{24n^2}\notag\\
 &+\frac{g_{\mathrm{mc}}(1536x^6-1472x^4+176x^2-3)}{48n^3}
 +O\!\left(\frac{1+|x|^8}{n^4}\right).
\end{align}
Substitution of \eqref{eq:cubic-moments} cancels the \(n^{-1}\) and
\(n^{-2}\) expectations and yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cubic-H-expectation}
 \E\mathcal H_{n,K_n}
 =\frac{3g_{\mathrm{mc}}}{16n^3}+O(n^{-4}).
\end{equation}
But
\[
 \frac{\Delta_n+(\tau_{\mathrm{mc}}-1)p_n}{T_n}
 =\E\mathcal H_{n,K_n},
 \qquad
 \frac{q_n}{T_n}=\frac{g_{\mathrm{mc}}}{2}(1+O(n^{-1})),
\]
so \eqref{eq:kappa-n-tau-def} gives
\eqref{eq:cubic-coeff-scaling}.

For the Bessel side, continue the logarithmic Hankel expansion by one further
term.  With \(\chi=4\nu^2\),
\begin{align}\label{eq:log-I-four-term}
 \log I_\nu(z)={}&z-\frac12\log(2\pi z)
 -\frac{\chi-1}{8z}-\frac{\chi-1}{16z^2}
 +\frac{(\chi-1)(\chi-25)}{384z^3}\notag\\
 &+\frac{(\chi-1)(\chi-13)}{128z^4}+O(z^{-5}).
\end{align}
The Cauchy-estimate argument used in \Cref{lem:large-argument-limit} permits
differentiation of the remainder.
At \(\nu=-1/12\), \(\tau=g_{\mathrm{mc}}/24\), substitution in
\eqref{eq:Dnu-kt-def} gives
\[
 D_{-1/12}^{(1,\tau_{\mathrm{mc}})}(z)
 =\frac{3}{32z^3}+O(z^{-4}),
\]
which is \eqref{eq:cubic-bessel-scaling}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \Cref{cor:wall-orientation}]
At \(\kappa=1\),
\[
 \tau_n(a,1)=1-\frac{\Delta_n/q_n}{p_n/q_n}.
\]
Dividing \eqref{eq:kappa-n-two-term} by
\eqref{eq:pq-ratio-two-term} gives
\eqref{eq:wall-orientation}.  The stated one-sided approach to
\(\tau_\infty\) follows immediately.  At \(a=a_{\mathrm{mc}}\), the same wall
line satisfies
\[
 p_n\{\tau_{\mathrm{mc}}-\tau_n(a_{\mathrm{mc}},1)\}
 =q_n\{1-\kappa_{n,\tau_{\mathrm{mc}}}(a_{\mathrm{mc}})\}.
\]
Using \eqref{eq:cubic-coeff-scaling} and
\(q_n/p_n=g_{\mathrm{mc}}n/4+O(1)\), the reciprocal of
\eqref{eq:pq-ratio-two-term}, gives
\eqref{eq:critical-wall-orientation}.

For general \(\kappa\), the affine wall equation gives
\[
 \tau_n(a,\kappa)=\tau_n(a,1)-(\kappa-1)\frac{q_n}{p_n}.
\]
Since \(q_n/p_n=gn/4+O_a(1)\), this proves
\eqref{eq:wall-transverse}; taking \(\kappa=1-x/n\) gives
\eqref{eq:wall-tangent-cone}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:jet-transfer-factor}
The first three verified wall jets obey one transfer rule.  If a
microcanonical threshold has leading form
\(1-\kappa_{n,\tau}\sim A_r^{\mathrm{mic}}n^{-r}\) and the corresponding
canonical Bessel wall has
\(D_{a-1}^{(1,\tau)}(z)\sim A_r^{\mathrm{can}}z^{-r}\), then the three cases
\(r=1,2,3\) proved above satisfy
\(A_r^{\mathrm{can}}=2^{1-r}A_r^{\mathrm{mic}}\).
Indeed, \(n\sim2z\) and the pointwise \(\kappa\)-deformation enters as
\(-2(1-\kappa)\), so this factor is exactly what canonical--microcanonical
matching predicts.  The successive instances are
\[
 c_\tau\leftrightarrow c_\tau,\qquad
 2(11/12-a)\leftrightarrow11/12-a,\qquad
 \frac38\leftrightarrow\frac3{32}.
\]
\end{remark}

\section{Relation to existing positivity theories}\label{sec:context}

The results combine several classical one-dimensional positivity mechanisms but
are not reducible to any one of them.

\subsection{Reciprocal-gamma and hypergeometric Tur\'anians}\label{subsec:reciprocal-gamma-hypergeometric-turanians}

The scalar quantity \(A=Z_a^2-ZZ_{aa}\) is the infinitesimal symmetric-shift
Tur\'anian of the reciprocal-gamma series:
\[
 Z(a+\delta,\lambda)Z(a-\delta,\lambda)-Z(a,\lambda)^2
 =-A(a,\lambda)\delta^2+O(\delta^4).
\]
Its coefficient sign is the infinitesimal form of the reciprocal-gamma Tur\'an
inequalities of Kalmykov and Karp
\cite[Thm.~3.1 and Ex.~1]{KalmykovKarp2013Reciprocal}; related gamma-ratio and
hypergeometric inequalities appear in
\cite{KalmykovKarp2013Ratios,KalmykovKarp2014LogConcavity,KalmykovKarp2017LogConcavity,KarpSitnik2010LogConvexity,KarpZhang2024LogConcavity}.
Those are scalar parameter-shift inequalities.  Here the new object is the
coupled determinant \(AC-\psi_1(a)B^2\), and the two deformation parameters
separate an affine coefficientwise boundary from a distinct pointwise boundary.
The intermediate region in which the determinant has exactly one positive
zero, and the high-degree critical law, have no scalar parameter-shift analogue
in these results.

\subsection{Bessel ratios, Tur\'an inequalities, and order derivatives}\label{subsec:bessel-ratios-turan-inequalities}

By \Cref{prop:scalar-H},
\[
 H_\nu
 =z^2\frac{I_{\nu+1}^2-I_\nu I_{\nu+2}}{I_\nu^2}>0
\]
is exactly a classical shifted modified-Bessel Tur\'an inequality, equivalently
the Amos-type lower ratio bound \eqref{eq:Amos-bound-exact}.  Amos established
sharp ratio bounds and monotonicity properties for nonnegative order
\cite{Amos1974Ratios}; N\aa sell developed convergent rational bounds on ranges
extending below zero \cite{Nasell1978Rational}; Segura linked sharp ratio bounds
to Tur\'an inequalities and later classified best simple algebraic bounds
\cite{Segura2011BoundsRatios,Segura2023BestBounds}; Hornik and Gr\"un gave a
systematic classification of Amos-type bounds on the full positivity range
\(\nu\ge-1\) \cite{HornikGrun2013Amos}.  Their Theorem~3 characterizes the
lower Amos-type bounds \(t/(\alpha+\sqrt{t^2+\beta^2})\) for \(R_\nu\) as those
with \(\alpha\ge\nu+\tfrac12\), \(\alpha+\beta\ge2(\nu+1)\) and \(\beta\ge0\);
the diagonal \(\alpha=\beta=\nu+\kappa\) of that family is admissible exactly
when \(\kappa\ge1\), which is \eqref{eq:H-kappa-global}.  The present paper does
not improve these scalar ratio bounds.  It embeds one classical scalar Tur\'an
direction into a new mixed matrix inequality.

The other raw diagonal entry,
\(G_\nu=-\partial_\nu^2\log I_\nu\), is strict log-concavity in the order and
is likewise known on \((-1,\infty)\)
\cite[Ex.~1]{KalmykovKarp2013Reciprocal},\cite[Thm.~3]{Nanthanasub2019LogConcavity}.  Order derivatives of Bessel
functions have been studied independently through closed forms, integral
representations, reflection identities, and large-order asymptotics
\cite[\S10.38]{NISTDLMFOrder},\cite{Brychkov2016HigherDerivatives,Dunster2017OrderDerivatives,GonzalezSantander2018ClosedForm,GonzalezSantander2023ReflectionFormulas}.
What appears not to have an antecedent in these lines is the specific Schur
coupling of continuous order curvature, mixed order--argument curvature, and
the corrected classical Tur\'an direction, together with its
\((\kappa,\tau)\) phase geometry and coefficientwise critical scaling.

\subsection{The Bessel distribution and conditioning hierarchy}\label{subsec:bessel-distribution-conditioning-hierarchy}

A recent general probabilistic approach to positive special-function series
normalizes their summands into probability laws and expresses parameter
log-derivatives through moments, covariances, and stochastic-order criteria,
with applications including Tur\'an inequalities \cite{Derbazi2026SignRule}.
That methodology is a close neighbor of the one-copy probabilistic viewpoint
here; the conditioned two-copy Schur fibers, four-copy determinant sectors,
and their phase geometry are additional structures specific to the present
problem.

The one-copy law \eqref{eq:bessel-law} is the discrete Bessel distribution
studied by Yuan and Kalbfleisch and later by Devroye
\cite{YuanKalbfleisch2000Bessel,Devroye2002Bessel}.  In that language the
classical scalar inequality \(H_\nu>0\) is underdispersion,
\(\operatorname{Var}Y<\E Y\), equivalent to the lower ratio bound in
\cite[eq.~(A.5)]{YuanKalbfleisch2000Bessel}.  The present paper uses this law
at two further levels: the coefficient fibers are two-copy Bessel laws
conditioned on their total, and determinant coefficients are four-copy laws
conditioned on the grand total.  Power-series distributions and fixed-sum conditioning are classical
\cite{Canton2026PowerSeries,Patil1962PowerSeries,PatilSeshadri1964Characterization};
the new content here is the resulting Bessel Schur-deficit fiber, its
mixed-determinant hierarchy, and the associated phase geometry.  Averaging the
conditioned fibers gives the covariance-deficit matrix
\eqref{eq:covariance-deficit-matrix}.  We have not found this conditioned
Schur-deficit hierarchy or the resulting strict digamma/count covariance
inequality \eqref{eq:covariance-ineq} stated in the discrete
Bessel-distribution literature.

\subsection{Total positivity and determinant hierarchies}\label{subsec:total-positivity-determinant-hierarchies}

Classical Bessel Tur\'an determinants, Hankel determinants, and determinant
monotonicity have been studied in several forms
\cite{BariczPogany2014TuranDeterminants,BustozIsmail1997TuranOrthogonal,IsmailLaforgia2007Monotonicity,MezoBaricz2017Turanian}.
A recent preprint of Salazar proves strict total positivity of the evaluation
kernel \((z,\nu)\mapsto I_\nu(z)\) for \(z>0\), \(\nu\ge0\), extending
integer-order results of Buchstaber and Glutsyuk
\cite{Salazar2026Darboux,BuchstaberGlutsyuk2019TotalPositivity}; a companion
preprint develops Hankel and Tur\'an determinant hierarchies from positive
moment representations \cite{Salazar2026Hankel}.  These are evaluation-minor
or Hankel-moment structures, not the mixed differential Schur determinant here.
There is also a simple covariance obstruction to identifying our determinant
with a rescaling-covariant confluent total-positivity minor.  Write
\(F(\nu,y)=\log I_\nu(e^y)\), so \(\partial_y=\Thetaz\).  Replacing
\(I_\nu(e^y)\) by \(e^{cy}I_\nu(e^y)\) leaves \(G_\nu\) and \(P_\nu\) fixed
but sends \(H_\nu\) to \(H_\nu+2c\), hence
\(\widetilde D_\nu=D_\nu+2cG_\nu\).
Since \(G_\nu>0\), the sign can be changed at any fixed point by choosing
\(c\) sufficiently negative.  By contrast, the signs of total-positivity
minors are preserved under positive row and column rescalings.  The same is
true for confluent \(y\)-jets: multiplication by \(e^{cy}\) sends
\((K,K_y,\ldots,K_{y^r})\) through an upper-triangular change of jet coordinates
with positive diagonal \(e^{cy}\), so every such confluent minor changes by a
positive factor.  Any canonical minor interpretation of the present inequality
must therefore incorporate additional Bessel-specific normalization or
nonlocal information.

\subsection{Canonical--microcanonical transfer}\label{subsec:canonical-microcanonical-transfer}

The identity \eqref{eq:D-canonical-average} is stronger than a numerical
coincidence between coefficient and pointwise asymptotics: the Bessel
determinant is the canonical average of its microcanonical determinant
sectors.  The concentration law \eqref{eq:ensemble-equivalence} identifies the
fixed total with \(n\sim2z\), while \Cref{thm:wall-fan,thm:cubic-multicritical}
show that the coefficient-wall and Bessel-wall jets agree through the first
three nontrivial critical orders.

A related preprint of the author \cite{Shields2026Cubic} proves
coefficientwise Schur-concavity for cubic multiple-Pochhammer series by a
beta-binomial fixed-total representation.  There the degree-\(m\)
coefficient of a product is a scalar microcanonical partition function for two
canonical index variables conditioned on their sum, and summation over \(m\)
recovers the ordinary pointwise Tur\'an inequality.  The present
reciprocal-gamma problem may be viewed as a matrix-valued counterpart: the
fixed-total fibers carry a two-direction Schur deficit, and determinant
coefficients require the additional four-copy polarization.  The two settings
therefore suggest that fixed-total positivity is a reusable mechanism rather
than a feature specific to the Bessel family.

This transfer is different in kind from a sharper pointwise Bessel inequality
and suggests an ensemble-level mechanism that may persist for other positive
special-function partition functions.

\section{Concluding remarks}\label{sec:conclusion}

The common object in this paper is a Schur deficit viewed at three resolutions.
At fixed two-copy total it is a microcanonical coefficient fiber; at fixed
four-copy total, mixed-determinant polarization turns those fibers into a
determinant sector; and canonical mixing over the total count recovers the
pointwise Bessel determinant:
\[
 D_{a-1}^{(\kappa,\tau)}(2\sqrt\lambda)=\E_\lambda d_N^{(\kappa,\tau)}.
\]
Coefficientwise and pointwise positivity are therefore not parallel phenomena
but the microcanonical and canonical positivity problems for the same discrete
Bessel ensemble.

This viewpoint explains both the endpoint proof and the phase diagram.  At the
endpoint, the only fiber that can be indefinite is \(M_1\), and it can
self-interact under determinant polarization only in degree two; the apparent
infinite tail of possible failures collapses to one finite defect.  Under the
two-parameter deformation, the vacuum sector, the exposed one-particle sector,
and the thermodynamic sector control the three global contacts.  The remaining
coefficient walls form an affine fan whose thermodynamic accumulation point
sits strictly below the exposed coefficient boundary:
\[
 \tau_\infty(a)<\tau_{\mathrm{cw}}(a,1)<1.
\]

Finally, the concentration of the four-copy canonical total identifies the
microcanonical degree with the Bessel argument through \(n\sim2z\).  This turns
the coefficient saddle into the analytic shadow of equivalence of ensembles
and explains why the coefficient-wall and Bessel-wall asymptotics have the same
successive critical cancellations, culminating in cubic matching at a unique
point.  The mechanism suggests that fixed-total positivity, finite-defect
localization, and canonical--microcanonical transfer may persist beyond the
Bessel family.

The following problems test that mechanism.

\begin{question}\label{q:wright}
For the Wright-type family
\[
 Z_\rho(a,\lambda)=\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{\lambda^k}{k!\,\Gamma(a+\rho k)},
 \qquad \rho>0,
\]
for which \(\rho\) do the conditioned two-copy fibers admit explicit
Schur-deficit or Gram representations?  When is the degree-one wall exposed
over all higher sectors, and does the same canonical--microcanonical phase
separation persist?
\end{question}

\begin{question}\label{q:higher-rank}
Can classical mixed-discriminant positivity
\cite{Bapat1989MixedDiscriminants} be combined with coefficient-fiber
localization so that isolated indefinite fibers affect only finitely many
determinant degrees?  What replaces the exceptional degree \(2j\) in higher
rank?
\end{question}

\begin{question}\label{q:saddle-transfer}
For
\[
 \mathcal A_f(\theta;x)=\sum_{n\ge0}f_na_n(\theta)x^n,
 \qquad f_n\ge0,\quad a_n(\theta)>0,
\]
which kernel conditions and shape conditions on \(f\) make every nonzero
fixed-total sector
\[
 [x^m]\,\mathcal A_f(u;x)\mathcal A_f(v;x)
\]
Schur-concave at fixed \(u+v\)?  When does this scalar property admit a
matrix Schur-deficit lift, and when do its microcanonical wall jets match the
canonical pointwise wall jets to all orders?  A related preprint of the author
\cite{Shields2026Cubic} proves the scalar first step for the cubic
multiple-Pochhammer kernel with nonnegative log-concave \(f\) of interval
support, using beta-binomial stochastic order.
\end{question}

\begin{question}\label{q:tp-origin}
Is there a canonical normalization, bordered kernel, or nonlocal minor for
\((z,\nu)\mapsto I_\nu(z)\) whose positivity is equivalent to the
Bessel--Schur inequality?  The rescaling obstruction in
\Cref{subsec:total-positivity-determinant-hierarchies} rules out a purely local
confluent-total-positivity explanation.
\end{question}

\begin{question}\label{q:bessel-statistics}
Can the covariance-deficit identity
\eqref{eq:microcanonical-covariance-deficit} be realized by a natural
augmented discrete-Bessel model in which \(\widehat M_m^{(\kappa,\tau)}\) and
its canonical average are genuine observed- or Fisher-information matrices,
rather than only information-deficit analogues?
\end{question}

\section{The exposed one-particle wall}\label{app:boundary-proof}

The only lengthy algebraic verification needed for the exposed-wall argument is
the boundary lemma used in \Cref{sec:phase}.  We give the details here so that
the main narrative retains the geometric content of the proof.

\begin{proof}[Proof of \Cref{lem:boundary-positivity}]
We establish four facts about \(\widehat N_m\).

\emph{1.  Positive definiteness for \(m\ge2\).}
Apply \Cref{thm:gram} with \(s=s_*\), so that \(\widehat N_m=N_m(s_*)\) and it suffices to show that
\[
 \widehat\rho_m
 =\rho_m(s_*)
 =s_*+c_m-\left(a+\frac m2-1\right)^2\psi_1(a+m-1)
\]
is positive.
By \eqref{eq:trig-upper-half},
\[
 \widehat\rho_m>\Lambda_m
 :=s_*+c_m-
 \frac{(a+m/2-1)^2}{a+m-3/2}.
\]
Treating \(m\) as a real variable,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Lm-derivative}
 \frac{\partial \Lambda_m}{\partial m}
 =\frac{2a-1}{2(2a+2m-3)^2}.
\end{equation}
If \(0<a<1/2\), then \(\Lambda_m\) decreases and
\[
 \lim_{m\to\infty}\Lambda_m
 =-\frac{8a^3g-6a^2g-8a^2+3}{4(2a^2g-1)}.
\]
The lower bound \eqref{eq:trig-lower} makes the numerator strictly smaller than
\(-2a\), so the limit is positive.  If \(a\ge1/2\), then \(\Lambda_m\) increases and
its minimum is
\[
 \Lambda_2=-\frac{4a^4g-4a^3-2a^2g-2a^2+a+1}
 {(2a+1)(2a^2g-1)}.
\]
For \(1/2\le a\le1/\sqrt2\), the coefficient of \(g\) in the numerator is
nonpositive and \eqref{eq:trig-lower} bounds that numerator above by \(-a\).
For \(a\ge1/\sqrt2\), the coefficient is nonnegative and
\eqref{eq:trig-upper-cubic} bounds it above by
\(-(a^2+1)/(3a)\).  Thus \(\Lambda_2>0\) in both cases.  Hence
\(\widehat\rho_m>0\) for every \(m\ge2\), so \Cref{thm:gram} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Nhat-m-pd}
 \widehat N_m\succ0\qquad(m\ge2).
\end{equation}

\emph{2.  Pairs involving \(\widehat N_0\).}
Set
\(F_m=\MD(\widehat N_0,\widehat N_m)\).  The definition of \(s_*\) gives
\(F_1=0\).  Using
\(\alpha_m-\alpha_1=-\sum_{r=1}^{m-1}(a+r)^{-2}\) and the explicit formulas for
\(\beta_m,c_m\), one obtains
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Fm-boundary}
 F_m
 =gc_m-s_*\sum_{r=1}^{m-1}\frac1{(a+r)^2}
 +\frac{(a-1)(m-1)}{a(a+m-1)}.
\end{equation}
If \(0<a<1/2\), then \(s_*<0\).  Dropping the positive middle term and using
\(g>a^{-2}\) shows that \(F_m>0\) once
\[
 m(a+m-1)>2a(1-a)(2a+2m-3).
\]
The difference between the two sides is increasing for \(m\ge2\), and at
\(m=2\) equals \(2(2a^3-a^2+1)>0\).

At \(a=1/2\), one has \(s_*=0\) and, for every \(m\ge2\),
\[
 F_m=\frac{\pi^2m}{8}-\frac{2(m-1)}{2m-1}>0.
\]
Assume now that \(a>1/2\).  The telescoping estimate
\[
 \frac1{x^2}
 <\frac1{(x-1/2)(x+1/2)}
 =\frac1{x-1/2}-\frac1{x+1/2}
\]
gives
\[
 \sum_{r=1}^{m-1}\frac1{(a+r)^2}
 <\frac{m-1}{(a+1/2)(a+m-1/2)}.
\]
Using \(c_m=m(m-1)/\{2(2a+2m-3)\}\) in
\eqref{eq:Fm-boundary}, it is therefore enough to prove
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Bm-boundary}
 \mathcal B_{a,m}(g)
 :=\frac{gm}{2(2a+2m-3)}
 -\frac{s_*}{(a+1/2)(a+m-1/2)}
 +\frac{a-1}{a(a+m-1)}>0.
\end{equation}
Put
\[
 \tilde a=a-\frac12>0,\qquad \tilde m=m-2\ge0,\qquad
 g_0=\frac1a+\frac1{2a^2}.
\]
The lower bound \eqref{eq:trig-lower} gives \(g>g_0\), and therefore
\[
 2a^2g-1>2a,
 \qquad
 s_*=\frac{a(2a-1)}{2a^2g-1}<a-\frac12=\tilde a.
\]
Substituting these two strict bounds into \eqref{eq:Bm-boundary} gives
\[
 \mathcal B_{a,m}(g)>
 \frac{\mathcal P(\tilde a,\tilde m)}
 {(\tilde a+1)(2\tilde a+1)^2(\tilde a+\tilde m+1)(\tilde a+\tilde m+2)(2\tilde a+2\tilde m+3)},
\]
where
\begin{align*}
 \mathcal P(\tilde a,\tilde m)={}&2\tilde a^4\tilde m+8\tilde a^4+4\tilde a^3\tilde m^2+23\tilde a^3\tilde m+26\tilde a^3\\
 &+2\tilde a^2\tilde m^3+19\tilde a^2\tilde m^2+48\tilde a^2\tilde m+35\tilde a^2\\
 &+4\tilde a\tilde m^3+22\tilde a\tilde m^2+40\tilde a\tilde m+25\tilde a\\
 &+2\tilde m^3+9\tilde m^2+14\tilde m+8>0.
\end{align*}
Thus \(F_m>0\).

\begin{equation}\label{eq:N0Nm-positive-boundary}
 \MD(\widehat N_0,\widehat N_m)>0\qquad(m\ge2).
\end{equation}

\emph{3.  Pairs involving \(\widehat N_1\).}
If \(a\ge1/2\), put \(t=\psi_1(a+1)\), so \(g=t+a^{-2}\).  Directly,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:det-N1-boundary}
 \det\widehat N_1
 =\frac{(2a-1)(2a^2t-2a+1)}{4a^2(2a^2t+1)}\ge0.
\end{equation}
The second factor is strictly positive because \eqref{eq:trig-lower} gives
\[
 2a^2t-2a+1>\frac1{(a+1)^2}.
\]
Thus \(\widehat N_1\succeq0\), and \eqref{eq:Nhat-m-pd} plus
\Cref{lem:MD-positive} gives
\(\MD(\widehat N_1,\widehat N_m)>0\) for \(m\ge2\).

If \(0<a<1/2\), then \(s_*<0\), \(\beta_1<0<\beta_m\) for \(m\ge2\), and
\(s_*+c_m>0\).  The last assertion follows from discrete monotonicity of \(c_m\)
and
\[
 s_*+c_2
 =\frac{4a^3+2a^2g-a-1}{(2a+1)(2a^2g-1)}>0
\]
by \eqref{eq:trig-lower}.  It remains to control
\(s_*\alpha_m-2\beta_1\beta_m\).  This quantity is positive precisely when
\[
 \beta_m(2a^2g-1)>a^2\alpha_m.
\]
For \(a<1\), the left side increases with \(m\) and the right side decreases,
so it is enough to take \(m=2\).  With \(t=\psi_1(a+1)\), the difference at
\(m=2\) is
\[
 \frac{a(2a+1)-a^2(1-a^2)t}{(a+1)^2}
 >\frac{a(2a^3+4a^2+2a+1)}{(2a+1)(a+1)^2}>0,
\]
where \eqref{eq:trig-upper-half} was applied to \(t\).  Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:N1Nm-positive-boundary}
 \MD(\widehat N_1,\widehat N_m)>0\qquad(m\ge2)
\end{equation}
in all cases.

\emph{4.  The degree-two self-pair.}
The only remaining possible negative contribution is the self-pair of
\(\widehat N_1\).  Again write \(t=\psi_1(a+1)\).  Direct substitution into the
coefficient formula gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:boundary-delta2}
 \widehat\Delta_2
 =\frac{(a^2t+1)\mathcal P_2(a,t)}
 {\Gamma(a)^4a^5(a+1)^3(2a^2t+1)},
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align*}
 \mathcal P_2(a,t)={}&
 (2a^5+4a^4+2a^3)t^2\\
 &+(8a^5+12a^4+5a^3+2a^2+a)t\\
 &-8a^4-6a^3+a^2+4a+2.
\end{align*}
This polynomial is strictly increasing in \(t>0\).  At the lower bound from
\eqref{eq:trig-lower},
\[
 t>\frac1{a+1}+\frac1{2(a+1)^2},
\]
one obtains
\[
 \mathcal P_2(a,t)>
 \frac{8a^5+20a^4+28a^3+30a^2+19a+4}{2(a+1)^2}>0.
\]
Thus \(\widehat\Delta_2>0\).

Finally, the congruence between \(M_m^{(1,\tau_*)}\) and \(\widehat N_m\)
preserves the signs of all mixed determinants.  The degree-one coefficient is
zero because \(\MD(\widehat N_0,\widehat N_1)=0\).  For \(n\ge3\), every pair in
the polarized coefficient formula is positive by
\eqref{eq:Nhat-m-pd}, \eqref{eq:N0Nm-positive-boundary}, and
\eqref{eq:N1Nm-positive-boundary}; degree two was handled separately above.
This proves the lemma.
\end{proof}

\section{Beyond the positive-series domain}\label{app:continuation}

The phase diagram and critical scaling of the preceding sections use the
positive-series domain \(a>0\) in an essential way.  We test what survives when the order crosses its endpoint
\(\nu=-1\).  The behavior changes qualitatively almost immediately.  On the open set
\[
 \mathcal U=\bigl\{(\nu,z)\in
 (\R\setminus\{-1,-2,\ldots\})\times(0,\infty):I_\nu(z)\ne0\bigr\},
\]
define \(G_\nu\), \(P_\nu\), \(H_\nu\), and \(D_\nu\) by
\eqref{eq:Gnu}--\eqref{eq:Dnu-def} with \(\log I_\nu\) replaced by \(\log|I_\nu(z)|\);
the order derivatives are taken on \(\mathcal U\), and for each fixed \(\nu\) the
resulting formulas restrict to any connected component of
\(\{z>0:I_\nu(z)\ne0\}\).  Where \(I_\nu>0\) this agrees with the ordinary
logarithm.

For every real \(a\notin\{0,-1,-2,\ldots\}\), the partial-fraction expansion
\begin{equation}\label{eq:trigamma-partial-fraction}
 \psi_1(a)=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\frac1{(a+r)^2}
\end{equation}
holds and shows that \(g=\psi_1(a)>0\) \cite[eq.~5.15.1]{NISTDLMFGamma}.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:coefficient-continuation}
For each fixed coefficient index, the scalar coefficient identities in
\eqref{eq:ABC-expansions}--\eqref{eq:cm-kappa-general}, and every resulting
finite-convolution formula for a coefficient of \(\Delta\), extend
meromorphically in \(a\).  A formula containing the product
\(S_mc_m^{(\kappa)}\) is understood as that complete product; its apparent pole
at \(2a+2m-3=0\) is removable.  If
\(a\in\R\setminus\{0,-1,-2,\ldots\}\), \(\lambda>0\), and
\(Z(a,\lambda)\ne0\), then \eqref{eq:ABC-log}, \eqref{eq:bessel-congruence},
and \eqref{eq:D-Delta} remain valid, with the positive real square root of
\(\psi_1(a)\) and with logarithmic derivatives interpreted through
\(\log|Z|\) and \(\log|I_{a-1}|\) on the relevant nonvanishing component.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By \Cref{lem:continuation}, \(Z\) and all fixed-order derivatives used here are
entire in \((a,\lambda)\).  For each fixed coefficient index, both sides of
each scalar coefficient identity are meromorphic functions of \(a\) and agree
for \(a>0\); the one-variable identity theorem extends them throughout their
common domain.  Each coefficient of \(\Delta=AC-gB^2\) is a finite convolution
of such scalar coefficient functions and is therefore meromorphic as well.
The zero of \(S_m\) cancels the apparent pole in
\(S_mc_m^{(\kappa)}\).

On the real nonpole set, \eqref{eq:trigamma-partial-fraction} gives
\(g=\psi_1(a)>0\), so the positive real square root used in the matrix
formulas is well defined.  On each connected component where \(Z\ne0\), \(Z\) has
constant sign and \(\partial_a\log|Z|=Z_a/Z\), so the differential identities
remain valid.  Finally, \eqref{eq:I-Z} gives
\[
 \log|I_{a-1}(2\sqrt\lambda)|
 =\frac{a-1}{2}\log\lambda+\log|Z(a,\lambda)|,
\]
which transfers the same identities to the Bessel side.
\end{proof}

Away from the poles the leading small-\(z\) term keeps its positive sign.  For
\(a=\nu+1\in\R\setminus\{0,-1,-2,\ldots\}\) the ascending series gives
\(I_\nu(z)=(z/2)^\nu\Gamma(\nu+1)^{-1}(1+O(z^2))\), so \(I_\nu\) has constant sign
near \(z=0\); by \Cref{lem:coefficient-continuation}, \eqref{eq:Delta1-sharp} and
\eqref{eq:D-Delta} with \(Z(a,0)=1/\Gamma(a)\) and \(\lambda=z^2/4\) give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:small-z-D}
 D_\nu(z)=\frac{2\bigl(a\psi_1(a)-1\bigr)}{a^3\psi_1(a)}z^2+O(z^4)
 \qquad(z\downarrow0),
\end{equation}
whose coefficient is positive for every such \(a\): for \(a>0\) by
\eqref{eq:trig-lower}, and for negative nonintegral \(a\) because
\eqref{eq:trigamma-partial-fraction} makes \(a\psi_1(a)-1\) and \(a^3\psi_1(a)\) both
negative.  Coefficientwise positivity of \(\Delta\) nonetheless fails already on
the next interval where \(1/\Gamma(a)>0\): for \(-3<\nu<-2\) one has
\(a\in(-2,-1)\) and \(\Gamma(a)>0\).

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:negative-coeff-failure}
If \(-2<a<-1\), equivalently \(-3<\nu<-2\), then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:negative-Delta2}
 [\lambda^2]\Delta(a,\lambda)=\Delta_2(a)<0.
\end{equation}
Thus the coefficientwise positivity theorem for \(\Delta\) cannot be extended to
that interval.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By \Cref{lem:coefficient-continuation}, the identity \eqref{eq:Delta2-Q}--\eqref{eq:Qstar-decomp} persists on \(-2<a<-1\).  Set \(b=-a\in(1,2)\), so that \(a+1\in(-1,0)\).  The denominator in \eqref{eq:Delta2-Q} is then negative: \(a^6>0\) and \(\Gamma(a)^4>0\), while \((a+1)^3<0\).  It therefore suffices to show \(Q_*(a,t)>0\).  By \eqref{eq:trigamma-partial-fraction}, \(t=\psi_1(a+1)>0\) and
\[
 \mathcal R_a=t-\frac1{a+1}>\frac1{b-1}.
\]
The first term in \eqref{eq:Qstar-decomp} is positive.  The second and third terms satisfy
\begin{align*}
 &2b^2(b-1)^2(8b^2-3b+1)\mathcal R_a
 -2b(b-1)(5b-3)\\
 &\qquad>
 2b(b-1)\left[8b^3-3b^2-4b+3\right]>0.
\end{align*}
Indeed, the polynomial in brackets equals \(4\) at \(b=1\) and has derivative \(24b^2-6b-4>0\) for \(b\ge1\).  Hence \(Q_*(a,t)>0\), proving \eqref{eq:negative-Delta2}.
\end{proof}

A complete classification beyond \(\nu>-1\) appears to require separate
treatment.
