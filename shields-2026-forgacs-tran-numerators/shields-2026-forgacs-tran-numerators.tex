\begin{abstract}
Forg\'acs and Tran proved that the polynomial sequence generated by the
reciprocal of \(Q(t)+zt^r\), with \(Q\) having only positive real zeros, is
eventually real-rooted with all zeros in an explicit positive interval.  Their
Problem~14 asks what survives when a proper numerator, equivalently arbitrary
fixed polynomial initial conditions, is introduced.  No bound can be uniform
over all numerators, but for each fixed numerator the number of zeros off the
positive ray is bounded independently of the coefficient index, and eventually
the same is true outside the Forg\'acs--Tran interval.  Restricting the numerator
to the denominator curve produces a canonical univariate weight that carries its
asymptotic effect.  The eventual
defect is bounded linearly in the weight's degree, and the normalized zero
distribution is independent of the numerator; the same weight governs the
finer local zero geometry.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Forg\'acs and Tran studied polynomial sequences generated by rational functions
with denominator \(Q(t)+zt^r\), where \(Q\) has only positive real zeros.  Let
\(Q\in\R[t]\) satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Q-hypotheses}
 Q(0)>0,
 \qquad
 Q(t)=Q(0)\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(1-\frac{t}{x_j}\right),
 \qquad
 0<x_1\le\cdots\le x_n,
\end{equation}
where multiplicities are allowed, and let \(r\ge1\).  For the denominator-only
sequence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:H-generating}
 \frac1{Q(t)+zt^r}=\sum_{m\ge0}H_m(z)t^m,
\end{equation}
Forg\'acs and Tran proved that every sufficiently large \(H_m\) is real-rooted
and that all of its zeros lie in an explicit interval
\(I_{Q,r}=(a,b)\subset(0,\infty)\) \cite[Thm.~1]{Forgacs2017RationalDenominator}.
Their proof constructs a strictly increasing parameterization
\(z=z(\theta)\), \(0<\theta<\pi/r\), for which the two minimum-modulus
denominator zeros form the conjugate pair
\[
 t_\pm(\theta)=\tau(\theta)e^{\pm i\theta}.
\]
We call this pair \emph{principal}.  Its interference controls
\(H_m(z(\theta))\) and produces the positive zero bulk.

Problem~14 of their earlier paper asks what survives when the numerator is
allowed to vary \cite{Forgacs2016RationalGenerating}.  Fix a real bivariate
polynomial \(N(t,z)\) with \(\deg_tN<\deg_t(Q(t)+zt^r)\)
and define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:P-generating-intro}
 \frac{N(t,z)}{Q(t)+zt^r}=\sum_{m\ge0}P_m(z)t^m.
\end{equation}
The numerator is equivalently a fixed vector of polynomial initial conditions
for the denominator recurrence; \Cref{prop:initial-data} makes this precise.
The natural fixed-numerator question is whether the number of zeros of \(P_m\)
outside \((0,\infty)\) remains bounded as \(m\to\infty\).

No such bound can be uniform over all numerators.  If \(R(z)\in\R[z]\) and
\(N(t,z)=R(z)\), then \(P_m(z)=R(z)H_m(z)\),
so every zero of \(R\) occurs in every coefficient polynomial.  Thus the
uniform-in-numerator reading of Problem~14 has a negative answer.  The
fixed-numerator problem has a positive one.

\subsection{Main theorem}\label{subsec:intro-main}

\begin{theorem}[Fixed-numerator Forg\'acs--Tran theorem]\label{thm:main}
Let \(Q\in\R[t]\) be nonconstant and satisfy \eqref{eq:Q-hypotheses}.  Let
\(r\ge1\) and assume \(\max\{\deg Q,r\}>1\).
Let \(N\in\R[t,z]\setminus\{0\}\) satisfy \(\deg_tN<\deg_t(Q(t)+zt^r)\),
where \(\deg_t\) is the degree in \(t\) over \(\R[z]\), so that
\(\deg_t(Q(t)+zt^r)=\max\{\deg Q,r\}\), and define \(P_m\in\R[z]\) by
\eqref{eq:P-generating-intro}.

\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] There is a constant \(C=C(Q,r,N)\) such that every nonzero \(P_m\)
has at most \(C\) zeros outside \((0,\infty)\), counted with multiplicity.

\item[(ii)] If \(I_{Q,r}=(a,b)\) is the Forg\'acs--Tran interval defined in
\eqref{eq:ab-def}, then every sufficiently large \(P_m\) is nonzero, has at
most \(C\) zeros outside \(I_{Q,r}\), counted with multiplicity, and has at
least \(\deg P_m-C\) distinct zeros in \(I_{Q,r}\).  If \(\lambda_N\) is the
Laurent valuation in \Cref{lem:laurent-reduction}, then eventually
\[
 \deg P_m=\left\lfloor\frac{m-\lambda_N}{r}\right\rfloor,
\]
and hence \(\deg P_m=m/r+O_{Q,r,N}(1)\).

\item[(iii)] Write the canonical Laurent restriction as
\[
 N\!\left(t,-\frac{Q(t)}{t^r}\right)=t^{\lambda_N}B_N(t),
 \qquad B_N(0)\ne0.
\]
There are constants \(C_0=C_0(Q,r)\) and \(C_1=C_1(Q,r)\), independent of
\(N\), such that for all sufficiently large \(m\) the interval conclusions in
\emph{(ii)} hold with defect at most \(C_0+C_1\deg B_N\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

Conclusion \emph{(i)} holds at every index, while \emph{(iii)} is eventual.  The onset in
\emph{(iii)} can depend on the numerator; whether a comparable linear bound
holds at every index is left open in \Cref{q:quantitative}.

\subsection{Proof strategy}\label{subsec:intro-strategy}

Two reductions expose the numerator dependence.  First, the denominator curve
\(Q(t)+zt^r=0\) has coordinate ring \(\R[t,t^{-1}]\).  Restricting the
numerator to this curve gives canonically
\[
 N\!\left(t,-\frac{Q(t)}{t^r}\right)=t^{\lambda_N}B_N(t),
 \qquad B_N(0)\ne0,
\]
and \Cref{lem:laurent-reduction} shows that, after an explicitly bounded
transient, \(P_m\) is exactly a shifted coefficient of
\(B_N(t)/(Q(t)+zt^r)\).  Thus \(\lambda_N\) is an index shift, while the tail
zero geometry is carried by one fixed univariate weight \(B_N\).

Second, a contour-separated pole expansion retains only denominator roots
whose moduli can compete and absorbs all other poles into one exponentially
small remainder.  On the Forg\'acs--Tran interval the principal conjugate pair
is co-dominant, and the normalized coefficient takes the oscillatory form
\[
 2|W(\theta)|\cos\bigl((M+1)\theta-\psi(\theta)\bigr)+R_M(\theta),
\]
where \(M=m-\lambda_N\), \(W\) is the principal residue amplitude, and
\(\psi=\arg W\).  Bounded phase variation and endpoint separation give the
uniform discrepancy in the Forg\'acs--Tran angular parameter \(\theta\), from
which \Cref{thm:main} follows.  This is the conjugate-pair extraction and
phase-counting strategy used in
\cite{AlHamdani2022Chebyshev,Tran2015RootDistribution}.  Here the phase budget
is bounded uniformly over weights of a given degree, so the eventual defect
bound of \Cref{thm:main} depends on the numerator only through \(\deg B_N\).
Dividing the same discrepancy by the degree of \(P_m\) gives a limiting zero
distribution independent of the numerator.  On compact subarcs where \(W\ne0\), the same
expansion becomes exponentially accurate
and yields local strong-clock spacing.  On a uniquely dominant branch, a zero
of the corresponding residue amplitude instead attracts a finite packet of
coefficient zeros.  The full synthesis of these two spectral regimes appears
in \Cref{sec:conclusion}.

\subsection{Prior work}\label{subsec:intro-prior-work}

Beraha--Kahane--Weiss theory identifies limiting zero loci from competition
between dominant branches and from zeros of dominant residue amplitudes
\cite{Beraha1975LimitsZeroes,Sokal2004ChromaticRoots}; Brown and Otto recently
extended this limit-set theory beyond the simple-characteristic-root setting
\cite{BrownOtto2025BKW}.  For rational generating functions, Tran developed
this viewpoint in several families \cite{Tran2014Discriminants,Tran2015RootDistribution}.
A circulating manuscript of Boyer and Tran treats a general numerator for
\(D(t,z)=A(z)t^n+B(z)t+1\) and obtains an algebraic limiting curve together
with a finite exceptional set controlled by a resultant
\cite[Thm.~1]{BoyerTran2012ZeroAttractor}.  These are asymptotic descriptions
of limit sets.  By contrast, \Cref{thm:main} bounds the exceptional zeros
uniformly in the coefficient index and, eventually, bounds the interval
discrepancy linearly in the degree of the canonical reduced weight.

Related bounded-defect phenomena occur for other recurrences and pencils.
Mai bounded the number of zeros leaving the limiting curve for a three-term
recurrence with a more general two-parameter family of initial conditions
\cite[Thm.~1]{Mai2018ExceptionalZeros}.  Luong and Tran obtained an explicit
numerator-degree bound for the four-term pencil
\(1+s+t+zst\) \cite{Luong2022FourTerm}, while Al Hamdani and Tran bounded the
number of zeros outside \((-1,1)\) for a Chebyshev-type generating function by
a conjugate-pair extraction and phase count \cite{AlHamdani2022Chebyshev}.
These results concern different denominator geometries, so they neither subsume
\Cref{thm:main} nor are subsumed by it.

Br\"and\'en and Leite have proved all-degree real-rootedness and interlacing
for the denominator-only Forg\'acs--Tran family through total nonnegativity
\cite[Thm.~4.5]{Branden2026TotallyNonnegative}, settling Problem~13 of
Forg\'acs and Tran \cite{Forgacs2016RationalGenerating}.  Their theorem is the
case \(N=1\); arbitrary fixed initial conditions can force nonreal or
nonpositive zeros, and \Cref{thm:main} says that this failure is confined to a
bounded defect.  In the special case \((\deg Q,r)=(2,1)\), the reduced problem
is classical quasi-orthogonality and admits the sharper all-index bound
\(\deg B+2\); see \Cref{rem:quadratic-case}.  The general denominator
recurrence need not have Favard three-term form.

\subsection{Organization}\label{subsec:intro-organization}

\Cref{sec:reduction} identifies proper numerators with polynomial initial
conditions, constructs the canonical Laurent weight, proves the eventual
degree formula, and records the elementary excluded case.  \Cref{sec:geometry}
separates the denominator geometry, contour expansion, and phase control of
the principal residue amplitude.  \Cref{sec:dominance} proves weighted
principal-pair dominance, converts it into uniform angular discrepancy, and
then proves \Cref{thm:main}.  \Cref{sec:consequences} develops the global and
local zero laws, including equidistribution, angular rigidity, strong-clock
spacing, and isolated exceptional attractors.  \Cref{sec:conclusion} gives the
spectral synthesis, discusses sharpness, and closes with the remaining
questions.

\section{Canonical Laurent reduction and eventual degree}\label{sec:reduction}

Throughout the global argument, put
\[
 D(t,z)=Q(t)+zt^r,
 \qquad
 d=\deg_tD=\max\{\deg Q,r\}.
\]
Apart from the elementary linear case in \Cref{prop:linear-case} and the local
cancellation result \Cref{prop:isolated-dominant-cancellation}, we assume
\eqref{eq:Q-hypotheses} with \(Q\) nonconstant, together with
\(\max\{\deg Q,r\}>1\); such a pencil is called
\emph{admissible}.  The condition \(r\ge1\) is essential to the polynomial
formulation, since for \(r=0\) the coefficients are generally rational in
\(z\).  Constant \(Q\) is excluded from the global theorem because then a
coefficient polynomial may have a zero of growing multiplicity at the origin,
and because \(g=-Q/t^r\) then has no critical point, so that neither
\(t_a\) nor \(I_{Q,r}\) exists.
For an admissible pencil, \(rQ(t)-tQ'(t)\) has a positive zero, hence \(t_a\),
and at \(r=1\) one has \(\deg Q\ge2\) and the negative zero \(t_b\), so that the
Forg\'acs--Tran interval \(I_{Q,r}\) exists; at \(\deg Q=r=1\) that polynomial
reduces to the nonzero constant \(Q(0)\).  The endpoints are recalled in
\Cref{sec:geometry}.

\subsection{Initial conditions}\label{subsec:initial-conditions}

The numerator carries exactly the same information as the polynomial initial
conditions of the denominator recurrence.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:initial-data}
Write \(D(t,z)=\sum_{j=0}^d d_j(z)t^j\), so that \(d_0=Q(0)\ne0\).  For each
\(N=\sum_{m=0}^{d-1}N_m(z)t^m\) with \(N_m\in\R[z]\), the coefficients \(P_m\) of
\(N/D\) satisfy
\[
 \sum_{j=0}^{m}d_j(z)P_{m-j}(z)=N_m(z)
 \qquad(0\le m<d).
\]
This is a lower-triangular system with constant diagonal \(d_0=Q(0)\), so
\(N\mapsto(P_0,\dots,P_{d-1})\) is a bijection from proper numerators, those with
\(\deg_tN<d\), onto
\(\R[z]^d\).  A fixed proper \(N\) is thus the same datum as a fixed vector
\((P_0,\dots,P_{d-1})\) of polynomial initial conditions.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Equating coefficients of \(t^m\) in \(N=D\sum_{k\ge0}P_kt^k\) gives the displayed
equations for \(0\le m<d\) and \(\sum_{j=0}^d d_j(z)P_{m-j}(z)=0\) for \(m\ge d\).
The first \(d\) equations express \((N_0,\dots,N_{d-1})\) from
\((P_0,\dots,P_{d-1})\) by a lower-triangular matrix with constant diagonal
\(d_0=Q(0)\ne0\), invertible over \(\R[z]\); the remaining equations then
determine \(P_m\) for \(m\ge d\).
\end{proof}

\subsection{Canonical Laurent reduction}\label{subsec:laurent-reduction}

The denominator curve itself has a canonical Laurent coordinate.  Put
\[
 g(t)=-\frac{Q(t)}{t^r}.
\]
Since \(Q(t)=Q(0)+tQ_1(t)\), the relation \(D=0\) gives
\[
 1=t\left(-\frac{Q_1(t)+zt^{r-1}}{Q(0)}\right),
\]
so \(t\) is a unit in the coordinate ring of \(D=0\).  Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{eq:denominator-coordinate-ring}
 \R[t,z]/(D)\cong\R[t,t^{-1}],
 \qquad
 z\longmapsto g(t).
\end{equation}
Indeed, in the quotient \(t\) is invertible and \(z=g(t)\), while the
substitution homomorphism to \(\R[t,t^{-1}]\) has kernel \((D)\) because \(D\)
is primitive and linear in \(z\).  The next lemma is the coefficient-theoretic
form of this identification.

\begin{lemma}[Canonical Laurent reduction]\label{lem:laurent-reduction}
Let \(N\in\R[t,z]\setminus\{0\}\) satisfy \(\deg_tN<d\), and define its
restriction to the denominator curve by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Laurent-restriction}
 L_N(t)=N(t,g(t))\in\R[t,t^{-1}].
\end{equation}
Then \(L_N\ne0\).  There are unique
\[
 \lambda_N\in\mathbb Z,
 \qquad
 B_N\in\R[t],\quad B_N(0)\ne0,
\]
such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:canonical-Laurent-factorization}
 L_N(t)=t^{\lambda_N}B_N(t).
\end{equation}
Moreover, there is \(S_N\in\R[t,t^{-1},z]\) with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:canonical-Laurent-division}
 N(t,z)=D(t,z)S_N(t,z)+t^{\lambda_N}B_N(t),
\end{equation}
and hence, for all sufficiently large \(m\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reduction-coeff}
 P_m(z)=[t^{m-\lambda_N}]
 \frac{B_N(t)}{D(t,z)}.
\end{equation}

More quantitatively, put
\[
 p_N=\deg_tN,\qquad E_N=\deg_zN,\qquad q=\deg Q.
\]
Then \eqref{eq:reduction-coeff} already holds for every
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reduction-threshold}
 m\ge E_N\max\{q-r,0\},
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reduced-degree-complexity}
 \deg B_N\le p_N+E_N\max\{q,r\}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If \(L_N\equiv0\), then \(N(t,z)\), viewed as a polynomial in \(z\) over
\(\R(t)\), vanishes at \(z=g(t)\).  Since \(D=t^r(z-g(t))\) is linear in \(z\),
\(D\) divides \(N\) in
\(\R(t)[z]\).  The polynomials \(Q(t)\) and \(t^r\) are coprime because
\(Q(0)\ne0\), so \(D\) is primitive in \(\R[t][z]\); Gauss's lemma gives
\(D\mid N\) in \(\R[t,z]\), contradicting \(\deg_tN<d\).  Thus \(L_N\ne0\),
and its least Laurent exponent gives the unique factorization
\eqref{eq:canonical-Laurent-factorization}.

In \(\R[t,t^{-1}][z]\), the polynomial \(N(t,z)-L_N(t)\) vanishes at
\(z=g(t)\), hence is divisible by \(z-g(t)=t^{-r}D(t,z)\).  This gives
\eqref{eq:canonical-Laurent-division}.  Dividing by \(D\), the Laurent
polynomial \(S_N\) contributes no coefficient of \(t^m\) for all sufficiently
large \(m\), which gives \eqref{eq:reduction-coeff}.

For the quantitative bounds, write
\[
 N(t,z)=\sum_{\beta=0}^{E_N}n_\beta(t)z^\beta.
\]
The difference quotient gives explicitly
\[
 S_N(t,z)
 =t^{-r}\sum_{\beta=1}^{E_N}n_\beta(t)
   \sum_{j=0}^{\beta-1}z^{\beta-1-j}g(t)^j.
\]
If \(q\le r\), then every Laurent exponent of \(S_N\) is at most
\(p_N-r\le-1\), so \eqref{eq:reduction-coeff} holds for every \(m\ge0\).  If
\(q>r\), then \(p_N\le q-1\), and the largest Laurent exponent in the displayed
sum is at most
\[
 p_N-r+(E_N-1)(q-r)\le E_N(q-r)-1.
\]
This proves \eqref{eq:reduction-threshold}.  Finally,
\(t^{rE_N}L_N(t)\) is a polynomial whose degree is at most
\[
 p_N+rE_N+E_N\max\{q-r,0\}=p_N+E_N\max\{q,r\}.
\]
Since \(t^{rE_N+\lambda_N}B_N=t^{rE_N}L_N\) and
\(rE_N+\lambda_N\ge0\), \eqref{eq:reduced-degree-complexity} follows.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:canonical-bounds-sharp}
The threshold and complexity bounds in \Cref{lem:laurent-reduction} are sharp
at the level of the displayed degree data.  If \(q>r\), take
\(N=t^{q-1}z^{E_N}\) with \(E_N\ge1\).  In the explicit difference quotient for
\(S_N\), the term with \(j=E_N-1\) has a nonzero Laurent term of exponent
\(E_N(q-r)-1\), so the reduction need not hold one index before
\eqref{eq:reduction-threshold}.  For the reduced-degree bound, fix \(E_N\ge1\).  If \(q>r\) and
\(0\le p_N<q\), take \(N=(1+t^{p_N})z^{E_N}\); then \(\lambda_N=-rE_N\) and
\(B_N=(-1)^{E_N}(1+t^{p_N})Q^{E_N}\), of degree \(p_N+E_Nq\).  If \(q\le r\) and
\(0\le p_N<r\), take \(N=t^{p_N}+c z^{E_N}\), with \(c\ne0\) chosen to avoid a possible cancellation of
the leading terms when \(q=r\) and \(p_N=0\); then \(\lambda_N=-rE_N\) and
\(\deg B_N=p_N+rE_N\).  Thus \eqref{eq:reduced-degree-complexity} is attained in
both degree regimes.
\end{remark}

Writing \(B_N(t)=\sum_jb_jt^j\) and expanding against
\eqref{eq:H-generating}, the reduced tail is therefore the fixed finite linear
combination
\begin{equation}\label{eq:P-linear-combination}
 P_m(z)=\sum_jb_jH_{m-\lambda_N-j}(z),
\end{equation}
where \(H_k=0\) for \(k<0\).  Thus the proof reduces to one fixed univariate
weight.  The canonical factorization also separates a pure index shift from
the spectral weight: if two proper numerators for the same denominator satisfy
\[
 L_{N_2}(t)=c\,t^sL_{N_1}(t),\qquad c\ne0,
\]
then their coefficient sequences satisfy
\[
 P_m^{(2)}(z)=c\,P_{m-s}^{(1)}(z)
\]
for all sufficiently large \(m\).  Thus \(\lambda_N\) controls only the
asymptotic index and degree shift, while the zero geometry of the tail is
carried, up to a nonzero scalar, by \(B_N\).

\subsection{Eventual degree}\label{subsec:eventual-degree}

From now on write
\begin{equation}\label{eq:F-M-def}
 F_M(z)=[t^M]\frac{B(t)}{Q(t)+zt^r},
 \qquad B\in\R[t],\quad B(0)\ne0.
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:eventual-degree}
For all sufficiently large \(M\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eventual-degree}
 \deg F_M=\left\lfloor\frac Mr\right\rfloor.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The geometric expansion
\begin{equation}\label{eq:geometric-expansion}
 \frac{B(t)}{Q(t)+zt^r}
 =\sum_{j\ge0}(-z)^jt^{rj}\frac{B(t)}{Q(t)^{j+1}}
\end{equation}
gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:F-coefficient-expansion}
 F_M(z)=\sum_{0\le j\le M/r}(-z)^j
 [t^{M-rj}]\frac{B(t)}{Q(t)^{j+1}}.
\end{equation}
Hence \(\deg F_M\le\lfloor M/r\rfloor\).

Put
\[
 \ell=\left\lfloor\frac Mr\right\rfloor,
 \qquad
 s=M-r\ell,
 \qquad 0\le s<r.
\]
The coefficient of \(z^\ell\) is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:leading-z-coeff}
 (-1)^\ell[t^s]\frac{B(t)}{Q(t)^{\ell+1}}.
\end{equation}
Let
\[
 \Lambda_Q=-\frac{Q'(0)}{Q(0)}=\sum_{j=1}^n\frac1{x_j}>0.
\]
For each fixed \(s\), expanding
\(Q(t)^{-(\ell+1)}=Q(0)^{-(\ell+1)}
\prod_{j=1}^n(1-t/x_j)^{-(\ell+1)}\) and extracting \(t^s\) shows that
\[
 Q(0)^{\ell+1}[t^s]\frac{B(t)}{Q(t)^{\ell+1}}
\]
is a polynomial in \(\ell\) of degree at most \(s\), with leading coefficient
\(B(0)\Lambda_Q^s/s!\).  It is therefore nonzero for all sufficiently large
\(\ell\), so the upper degree bound is attained.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:degree-attainment}
For \(B=1\), the bound \(\deg F_M\le\lfloor M/r\rfloor\) is
\cite[Lem.~8]{Forgacs2017RationalDenominator} and is attained at every \(M\),
since \(\prod_j(1-t/x_j)^{-(\ell+1)}\) has positive coefficients.  For a
general fixed weight the onset cannot be uniform even when \(\deg B=1\).
Indeed, take \(Q=1-t\), \(r=2\), and, for any integer \(L\ge1\),
\[
 B^{(L)}(t)=1-(L+1)t.
\]
At \(M=2L+1\) one has \(\ell=L\), \(s=1\), and the nominal leading
coefficient in \eqref{eq:leading-z-coeff} vanishes because
\[
 [t]\frac{B^{(L)}(t)}{(1-t)^{L+1}}=(L+1)-(L+1)=0.
\]
Thus \(\deg F_{2L+1}<L\), giving arbitrarily late degree drops among linear
weights, although \Cref{lem:eventual-degree} holds eventually for every fixed
\(B^{(L)}\).
\end{remark}

For the original numerator, \Cref{lem:laurent-reduction} and
\Cref{lem:eventual-degree} give the exact eventual formula
\begin{equation}\label{eq:exact-eventual-degree-shift}
 \deg P_m=
 \left\lfloor\frac{m-\lambda_N}{r}\right\rfloor,
\end{equation}
and hence \(\deg P_m=m/r+O_{Q,r,N}(1)\).

\subsection{The elementary linear case}\label{subsec:linear-case}

The excluded case \(\deg Q=r=1\) has no Forg\'acs--Tran interval, but the
bounded-defect conclusion is immediate.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:linear-case}
Suppose \(\deg Q=r=1\).  Then the first conclusion of \Cref{thm:main} holds:
there is a \(C\) such that every nonzero \(P_m\) has at most \(C\) zeros outside
\((0,\infty)\), counted with multiplicity.  Its second conclusion is vacuous
here, \(I_{Q,r}\) being undefined for \(\deg Q=r=1\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write
\[
 Q(t)=q_0+q_1t,
 \qquad q_0>0,
 \qquad q_1<0.
\]
The properness condition \(\deg_tN<1\) forces \(N(t,z)=R(z)\).  Hence
\begin{align*}
 \frac{R(z)}{q_0+(q_1+z)t}
 &=\sum_{m\ge0}
 \frac{(-1)^mR(z)(q_1+z)^m}{q_0^{m+1}}t^m,\\
 P_m(z)&=\frac{(-1)^m}{q_0^{m+1}}R(z)(z+q_1)^m.
\end{align*}
The moving zero \(-q_1\) is positive, and every exceptional zero belongs to
the fixed polynomial \(R\).
\end{proof}

\section{Spectral geometry, residues, and the principal amplitude}\label{sec:geometry}

The parameterization, minimum-modulus property, and endpoint expansions come
from \cite{Forgacs2017RationalDenominator}; the uniform separation estimates
and weighted consequences are the ingredients needed below.

\subsection{Forg\'acs--Tran geometry and endpoint separation}\label{subsec:FT-geometry}

Let
\[
 g(t)=-\frac{Q(t)}{t^r}.
\]
Following Forg\'acs and Tran, let \(t_a>0\) be the smallest positive critical
point of \(g\), and set \(b=+\infty\) when \(r>1\), while for \(r=1\) let \(t_b<0\) be
the unique negative critical point of \(g\) \cite[Lem.~5]{Forgacs2017RationalDenominator} and put
\(b=g(t_b)\).  This is the endpoint convention of \cite[Thm.~1]{Forgacs2017RationalDenominator},
where both endpoints are read off the polynomial
\(t^{r-1}\bigl(rQ(t)-tQ'(t)\bigr)\); its zero of order \(r-1\) at the origin is
what makes the upper endpoint unbounded for \(r>1\).
Set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ab-def}
 a=g(t_a),
 \qquad
 I_{Q,r}=(a,b).
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Forg\'acs--Tran geometry and endpoint separation]\label{thm:FT-geometry}
There are real-analytic functions
\[
 \tau:(0,\pi/r)\to(0,\infty),
 \qquad
 z:(0,\pi/r)\to I_{Q,r},
\]
with \(z\) strictly increasing, \(z(\theta)\to a\) as \(\theta\downarrow0\) and
\(z(\theta)\to b\) as \(\theta\uparrow\pi/r\), such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:principal-pair}
 t_\pm(\theta)=\tau(\theta)e^{\pm i\theta}
\end{equation}
are zeros of \(Q(t)+z(\theta)t^r\).  They are the only denominator zeros in
the closed disk \(|t|\le\tau(\theta)\).

On every compact subinterval of \((0,\pi/r)\), the remaining denominator
zeros are separated from the principal pair by a uniform modulus ratio.
Near each endpoint, write \(\zeta_j(\theta)=t_j(\theta)/\tau(\theta)\) for the
normalized denominator zeros.  At \(\theta\downarrow0\), a smallest zero \(x_1\)
of \(Q\) of multiplicity \(\rho\ge2\) produces a cluster of \(\rho\) zeros tending
to \(x_1\), whose \(\rho-2\) nonprincipal members satisfy a linear modulus
gap
\begin{equation}\label{eq:endpoint-linear-gap}
 |\zeta_j(\theta)|\ge1+c\delta,
\end{equation}
for some \(c>0\), \(\delta\) denoting the angular distance to the endpoint, while
every zero outside the cluster satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:endpoint-fixed-gap}
 |\zeta_j(\theta)|\ge1+c_*
\end{equation}
for a fixed \(c_*>0\).  When \(\rho=1\) the cluster is the principal pair alone
and every nonprincipal zero satisfies \eqref{eq:endpoint-fixed-gap}.  At
\(\theta\uparrow\pi/r\) with \(r>1\), the finite normalized cluster approaches the
\(r\)th roots of \(-1\) and its nonprincipal members satisfy
\eqref{eq:endpoint-linear-gap}; if \(\deg Q>r\), the remaining
\(\deg Q-r\) roots tend to infinity.  At \(\theta\uparrow\pi\) with \(r=1\), the principal pair coalesces at
\(t_b\) to a zero of multiplicity exactly two, and every other normalized zero
satisfies \eqref{eq:endpoint-fixed-gap}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The existence, strict monotonicity, and endpoint limits of \(\tau\) and \(z\)
are established in Lemmas~2--6 of \cite{Forgacs2017RationalDenominator}.
The minimum-modulus assertion is
\cite[Props.~1--2]{Forgacs2017RationalDenominator}.  Both exclude \((\deg Q,r)=(2,1)\), where
\(\tau\) is constant; that case is \Cref{rem:quadratic-case}.  Compact-uniform
separation follows by continuity of the roots, compactness, and the strict
pointwise modulus gap.

The endpoint expansions are the second and third cases in the proof of
\cite[Prop.~3]{Forgacs2017RationalDenominator}.  We extract the uniform
separation asserted above.  At the lower endpoint, a smallest zero of
multiplicity \(\rho\ge2\) gives normalized cluster roots
\[
 \zeta_j(\theta)=1+\frac{\cos(\pi/\rho)-\omega_j}{\sin(\pi/\rho)}\,\theta
 +O(\theta^2),\qquad\omega_j=e^{(2j-1)\pi i/\rho},
\]
so that
\[
 |\zeta_j(\theta)|=1+\frac{\cos(\pi/\rho)-\Re\omega_j}{\sin(\pi/\rho)}\,\theta
 +O(\theta^2);
\]
the linear coefficient vanishes exactly for the two principal indices
\(\omega_j=e^{\pm i\pi/\rho}\) and is strictly positive otherwise.  At the upper
endpoint for \(r>1\), with \(\eta=\pi/r-\theta\) and \(\omega_j\) now the \(r\)th roots of
\(-1\),
\[
 |\zeta_j(\theta)|=1+\frac{\cos(\pi/r)-\Re\omega_j}{\sin(\pi/r)}\,\eta+O(\eta^2),
\]
again with vanishing linear coefficient only for the principal pair
\(\omega_j=e^{\pm i\pi/r}\).  Since the clusters are finite, the positive linear
coefficients have a uniform positive minimum and the remainders are uniform
over the cluster, giving \eqref{eq:endpoint-linear-gap}.  For \(\rho=2\) the
cluster is the principal pair alone and
\eqref{eq:endpoint-linear-gap} is vacuous.

The zeros outside the cluster keep a fixed gap.  At the lower endpoint
\(\tau(\theta)\to t_a\) and \(z(\theta)\to a\).  Any denominator root that
does not remain bounded has modulus tending to infinity and hence, since
\(t_a>0\), satisfies \eqref{eq:endpoint-fixed-gap} after the endpoint
neighborhood is decreased.  Among the roots that remain bounded, the normalized
values converge to the
zeros of the limit polynomial obtained at \((\tau,z)=(t_a,a)\), namely \(\zeta=1\)
with multiplicity \(\max\{\rho,2\}\) together with the ratios \(t_j/t_a\) formed
from the remaining bounded zeros \(t_j\) of \(Q(t)+at^r\), each of modulus
exceeding \(t_a\).  The minimum-modulus assertion is quantified over
\(\theta\in(0,\pi/r)\) and in the limit gives only \(|t_j|\ge t_a\), so strictness
is supplied directly.  Let \(t_e\ne0\) and \(z_e\) be real with
\(Q(t_e)+z_et_e^{r}=0\).  For
\(|t|=|t_e|\) with \(t\ne t_e\) one has \(\Re t<t_e\) if \(t_e>0\) and
\(\Re t>t_e\) if \(t_e<0\), so
\[
 |t-x_j|^2-|t_e-x_j|^2=2x_j(t_e-\Re t)
 \qquad(1\le j\le n)
\]
has the sign of \(t_e\) for every \(j\); hence
\(|Q(t)|\ne|Q(t_e)|=|z_e|\,|t_e|^{r}=|z_et^{r}|\) and \(t\) is not a zero of
\(Q(t)+z_et^{r}\) \cite[Prop.~3]{Forgacs2017RationalDenominator}.  At the lower endpoint this
applies with \(t_e=t_a\) and \(z_e=a\).  For \(\rho\ge2\), both \(Q\) and
\(Q'\) vanish at \(x_1\), so \(x_1\) is a critical point of \(g\); as
\(tQ'(t)-rQ(t)<0\) on \((0,x_1)\), it is the smallest positive one.  Hence
\(t_a=x_1\) and \(a=g(x_1)=-Q(x_1)/x_1^{r}=0\), so the limit polynomial is
\[
 Q(0)(1-\zeta)^{\rho}\prod_{j>\rho}\left(1-\frac{x_1\zeta}{x_j}\right),
\]
whose zeros off \(\zeta=1\) are the fixed ratios \(x_j/x_1>1\).  Continuity of the
zeros in the coefficients gives \eqref{eq:endpoint-fixed-gap} for any \(c_*\)
below the least of these ratios diminished by one.

At the upper endpoint for \(r=1\) the comparison above applies with
\(t_e=t_b<0\) and \(z_e=b\), the sign in the display reversing with \(t_e\).  A
multiplicity above two at \(t_b\) would force \(Q''(t_b)=0\), whereas the zeros of
\(Q''\) are positive; the collision is therefore exactly double, and
\(\deg Q\ge2>r\) leaves no escaping zero, the leading \(t\)-coefficient of
\(Q(t)+zt\) being the nonzero leading coefficient of \(Q\), so the
remaining \(\deg Q-2\) zeros satisfy \eqref{eq:endpoint-fixed-gap} by continuity
of the zeros in the coefficients \cite[Prop.~3]{Forgacs2017RationalDenominator}.  The upper endpoint for \(r>1\) is the third case of the same proof.  If
\(\deg Q>r\), Rouch\'e's theorem on any fixed circle \(|t|=R\) gives exactly
\(r\) zeros inside for all sufficiently large \(z\), since \(zt^r\) dominates
\(Q(t)\) there.  Thus every remaining root tends to infinity, and in particular
stays outside the fixed separating contours used below.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:principal-endpoint-regularity}
The principal branch
\[
 \gamma(\theta):=t_+(\theta)=\tau(\theta)e^{i\theta}
\]
extends to a regular real-analytic arc on the closed parameter interval
\([0,\pi/r]\), with the upper endpoint interpreted by its finite limit
\(\gamma(\pi/r)=0\) when \(r>1\).

More precisely, at a finite endpoint let \(\delta\) denote angular distance to
the endpoint, let \(t_e\ne0\) be the limiting principal root, and let \(k\ge2\)
be its multiplicity as a zero of the limiting denominator.  Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:principal-finite-endpoint-regularity}
 \gamma(\theta)=t_e+\gamma_e\delta+O(\delta^2),
 \qquad
 \gamma_e\ne0,
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:z-endpoint-order}
 z(\theta)-z_e=c_e\delta^k(1+O(\delta)),
 \qquad
 c_e\ne0.
\end{equation}
At the upper endpoint when \(r>1\), with
\(\eta=\pi/r-\theta\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:principal-infinite-endpoint-regularity}
 \gamma(\theta)=\eta\,T(\eta),
 \qquad
 T(0)\ne0,
\end{equation}
for a real-analytic complex-valued function \(T\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
At a finite endpoint write
\[
 Q(t)+z_et^r=(t-t_e)^kG(t),
 \qquad
 G(t_e)\ne0.
\]
A nearby denominator root satisfies
\[
 (t-t_e)^k=-(z-z_e)\frac{t^r}{G(t)}.
\]
On the interior side write \(z-z_e=\pm y^k\), \(y\ge0\), with the sign fixed.
After choosing an analytic \(k\)th root of the nonvanishing factor on the
right, the \(k\) local branches have the form
\[
 t(y)=t_e+\gamma y+O(y^2),
 \qquad
 \gamma\ne0.
\]
The principal branch is nonreal for interior parameters.  Its
\(\gamma\) therefore has nonzero imaginary part: if \(\gamma\) were real,
conjugation and uniqueness of the local analytic branch would make \(t(y)\)
real for real \(y\).  Since \(t_e\) is real and nonzero,
\[
 \delta=\pm\Im\log\!\left(\frac{t(y)}{t_e}\right)
\]
has nonzero derivative at \(y=0\).  Inverting \(y\) as a real-analytic
function of \(\delta\) gives
\eqref{eq:principal-finite-endpoint-regularity}, and
\(z-z_e=\pm y^k\) gives \eqref{eq:z-endpoint-order}.

When \(r>1\), the upper endpoint has \(z\to+\infty\); put \(w=z^{-1/r}>0\),
the positive real root, and take the principal branch of \(Q(t)^{1/r}\) near
\(t=0\), which is available because \(Q(0)>0\).  From
\(t^r=-Q(t)/z\), each small root satisfies
\[
 t=\omega w\,Q(t)^{1/r},
 \qquad
 \omega^r=-1.
\]
The implicit-function theorem gives a branch analytic in \(w\) with
nonzero derivative at \(0\).  On the principal branch,
\(\eta=-r^{-1}\arg Q(t_+)\), and
\[
 \frac{\mathrm d\eta}{\mathrm dw}(0)
 =\frac{-Q'(0)}{rQ(0)}Q(0)^{1/r}\sin(\pi/r)>0,
\]
the sign holding because \(Q'(0)=-Q(0)\sum_{j=1}^nx_j^{-1}<0\).
Thus \(w\) is real-analytic in \(\eta\), which gives
\eqref{eq:principal-infinite-endpoint-regularity}.  The displayed expansions,
together with \(\gamma'(\theta)=e^{i\theta}(\tau'(\theta)+i\tau(\theta))\ne0\)
in the open interval, prove regularity on the closed arc.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:quadratic-case}
For \(\deg Q=2\) and \(r=1\), write
\(Q(t)=q_0+q_1t+q_2t^2\), where \(q_0,q_2>0\) and \(q_1<0\).  The two
denominator roots have product \(q_0/q_2\), so
\[
 \tau=\sqrt{q_0/q_2},
 \qquad
 z(\theta)=-q_1-2\sqrt{q_0q_2}\cos\theta,
 \qquad 0<\theta<\pi.
\]
Thus \(z\) is strictly increasing,
\[
 I_{Q,1}
 =(-q_1-2\sqrt{q_0q_2},-q_1+2\sqrt{q_0q_2}),
\]
the principal pair exhausts the denominator zeros, and both endpoint
collisions are exactly double.

This is also the classical quasi-orthogonal case.  With
\[
 p_m(z)=q_0(-q_0)^mH_m(z),
\]
coefficient comparison gives
\[
 p_m=(q_0q_2)^{m/2}
 U_m\!\left(\frac{z+q_1}{2\sqrt{q_0q_2}}\right).
\]
If \(B(t)=\sum_{j=0}^Kb_jt^j\) with \(b_0b_K\ne0\), then
\[
 F_M(z)=\frac1{q_0(-q_0)^M}
 \sum_{j=0}^{\min\{K,M\}}b_j(-q_0)^jp_{M-j}(z).
\]
For \(M\ge K\), Shohat's theorem in the form
\cite[Lem.~3.1]{Duran2026LinearCombinations} gives at least \(M-K\) distinct
real zeros of odd multiplicity in \(\overline{I_{Q,1}}\), hence at least
\(M-K-2\) in \(I_{Q,1}\).  Since \(\deg F_M=M\), at most \(K+2\) zeros lie
outside the open interval, counted with multiplicity; for \(M<K\) the same
bound is trivial.  Thus the reduced defect is at most \(\deg B+2\) at every
index.  The denominator recurrence of a general admissible pencil is not of
Favard three-term form.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Contour-separated residues}\label{subsec:contour-residues}

The core contour-pushing argument is the standard meromorphic coefficient
extraction of
\cite[Thm.~IV.10 and Note~IV.27]{Flajolet2009AnalyticCombinatorics}; the following parameter-uniform
form records what is needed here for an arbitrary rectifiable Jordan contour,
including holomorphy in \(z\) and the bookkeeping at multiple poles.

\begin{lemma}[Contour-separated pole expansion]\label{lem:contour-separation}
Let \(r\ge1\), let \(Q,B\in\C[t]\) with \(Q(0)\ne0\), and set
\[
 D(t,z)=Q(t)+zt^r,
 \qquad
 F_M(z)=[t^M]\frac{B(t)}{D(t,z)}.
\]
Let \(U\subset\C\), and let \(\Gamma\) be a positively oriented rectifiable
Jordan curve whose interior contains \(0\), such that
\(D(t,z)\ne0\) for \(t\in\Gamma\) and \(z\in U\).  If
\(\mathcal C(z)\) denotes the set of distinct zeros of \(D(\,\cdot\,,z)\) inside
\(\Gamma\), then for every \(M\ge0\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:contour-separated-expansion}
 F_M(z)
 =-\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal C(z)}
   \operatorname*{Res}_{t=\xi}
   \frac{B(t)}{t^{M+1}D(t,z)}
   +E_M(z),
 \qquad
 E_M(z)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_\Gamma
   \frac{B(t)}{t^{M+1}D(t,z)}\dd t.
\end{equation}
Each distinct pole contributes its residue once, with the residue at a multiple
zero understood in the usual higher-order sense.  If every root in
\(\mathcal C(z)\) is simple, its contribution is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:simple-residue-amplitude}
 -\frac{B(\xi)}{\partial_tD(\xi,z)}\,\xi^{-M-1}.
\end{equation}
If, moreover,
\[
 C_\Gamma=\sup_{z\in U,\,t\in\Gamma}
 \left|\frac{B(t)}{D(t,z)}\right|<\infty,
 \qquad
 R_\Gamma=\inf_{t\in\Gamma}|t|>0,
\]
then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:contour-remainder-bound}
 |E_M(z)|
 \le \frac{L_\Gamma C_\Gamma}{2\pi}\,R_\Gamma^{-M-1}
 \qquad(z\in U),
\end{equation}
where \(L_\Gamma\) is the length of \(\Gamma\).  Normalizing by any positive
scale bounded by \(\sigma R_\Gamma\) with \(\sigma<1\) therefore turns
\eqref{eq:contour-remainder-bound} into a bound of order \(\sigma^M\).  If \(U\) is open, then
\(E_M\) is holomorphic in \(z\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Take a small positively oriented circle about the origin containing no zero of
\(D(\,\cdot\,,z)\).  Cauchy's coefficient formula gives \(F_M\) as the integral
of \(B(t)/(t^{M+1}D(t,z))\) on that circle.  Expanding the contour to
\(\Gamma\) crosses exactly the poles in \(\mathcal C(z)\), and the residue
theorem gives \eqref{eq:contour-separated-expansion}.  Formula
\eqref{eq:simple-residue-amplitude} is the residue at a simple zero.  The
remainder estimate follows by bounding the integrand: on \(\Gamma\) one has
\(|B(t)/D(t,z)|\le C_\Gamma\) and \(|t|^{-M-1}\le R_\Gamma^{-M-1}\), while
\(\Gamma\) has length \(L_\Gamma\).  Holomorphicity follows by
differentiation under the integral sign.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The principal amplitude and its phase}\label{subsec:principal-amplitude}

For the fixed reduced numerator \(B\), at any simple denominator zero put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:residue-amplitude}
 \mathcal W(t,z)=-\frac{B(t)}{\partial_tD(t,z)}.
\end{equation}
For \(0<\theta<\pi/r\), define the weighted principal amplitude by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:W-def}
 W(\theta)=\mathcal W(t_+(\theta),z(\theta)).
\end{equation}
The denominator in \eqref{eq:residue-amplitude} at \(t=t_+\) equals
\(-t_+(\theta)^{r}g'(t_+(\theta))\), since
\(z(\theta)=-Q(t_+)/t_+^{r}\) and
\(tQ'(t)-rQ(t)=-t^{r+1}g'(t)\).  A double principal root would make
\(t_+(\theta)\) a critical point of \(g\), that is, would force
\(t_+Q'(t_+)=rQ(t_+)\).  This cannot occur.  The zeros of \(Q\) are positive,
so \(Q\) does not vanish off the real axis, and with \(x_1,\dots,x_n\) as in
\eqref{eq:Q-hypotheses}, listed with multiplicity,
\[
 \Im\frac{tQ'(t)}{Q(t)}
 =\Im\sum_{j=1}^n\frac t{t-x_j}
 =-\Im t\sum_{j=1}^n\frac{x_j}{|t-x_j|^2}
 <0
 \qquad(\Im t>0),
\]
whereas \(r\) is real.  Since \(t_+(\theta)=\tau(\theta)e^{i\theta}\) with
\(0<\theta<\pi/r\le\pi\) lies in the open upper half-plane,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:principal-simple}
 g'(\gamma(\theta))\ne0
 \qquad(0<\theta<\pi/r),
\end{equation}
where \(\gamma=t_+\) is the principal branch of
\Cref{lem:principal-endpoint-regularity}.  The principal root is therefore
simple on the open interval, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:W-on-g}
 W(\theta)=\frac{B(\gamma(\theta))}
 {\gamma(\theta)^{r}g'(\gamma(\theta))}
\end{equation}
is finite and real-analytic on \((0,\pi/r)\).

Remove the two principal residues from the normalized coefficient and define
\(R_M\) by the exact identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:principal-decomposition}
 \tau(\theta)^{M+1}F_M(z(\theta))
 =2\Re\!\left(W(\theta)e^{-i(M+1)\theta}\right)+R_M(\theta).
\end{equation}
When a contour contains only the principal pair,
\Cref{lem:contour-separation} identifies \(R_M\) with its normalized contour
remainder.  When a larger endpoint cluster must be retained, the same lemma
expresses \(R_M\) as the explicit residues of the other cluster members plus
that single contour remainder.  \Cref{fig:decomposition-and-defect}(A) shows
this decomposition for one weight, with an interior zero of the amplitude and
the endpoint windows of \eqref{eq:retained-range}.

\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:amplitude-divisor}
Let \(K=\deg B\).  The zeros of \(W\) in \((0,\pi/r)\) are precisely the
parameters for which \(B(\gamma(\theta))=0\).  If they are
\(\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_J\), with multiplicities
\(\nu_1,\ldots,\nu_J\), then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:amplitude-zero-count}
 \sum_{j=1}^J\nu_j\le K,
\end{equation}
and near \(\theta_j\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:W-local-zero}
 W(\theta)=(\theta-\theta_j)^{\nu_j}U_j(\theta),
 \qquad U_j(\theta_j)\ne0.
\end{equation}

Near either endpoint,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:W-endpoint-form}
 W(\theta)=\delta^{p}V(\delta),
\end{equation}
where \(\delta\) is angular distance to the endpoint, \(p\in\mathbb Z\), and
\(V\) is real-analytic and nonvanishing at \(0\).  At a finite endpoint where
the limiting principal root has denominator multiplicity \(k\) and \(B\)
vanishes there to order \(\nu\),
\[
 p=\nu-(k-1).
\]
Thus at the lower endpoint \(k=\max\{\rho,2\}\), where \(\rho\) is the
multiplicity of the smallest zero of \(Q\), and at the finite upper endpoint
\(r=1\) one has \(k=2\).  At the upper endpoint for \(r>1\), one has \(p=1\).

Consequently, on every component of
\((0,\pi/r)\setminus\{\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_J\}\), a continuous branch
\(\psi=\arg W\) satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phase-derivative-bound}
 |\psi'(\theta)|\le\kappa_{Q,r,B}
\end{equation}
for some finite constant \(\kappa_{Q,r,B}\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The principal root is simple in the open interval by
\eqref{eq:principal-simple}, and
\[
 \gamma'(\theta)=e^{i\theta}\bigl(\tau'(\theta)+i\tau(\theta)\bigr)\ne0.
\]
Hence composition with \(\gamma\) preserves the multiplicity of any zero of
\(B\).  Since \(\gamma\) is injective, the zeros of \(B\circ\gamma\) have total
multiplicity at most \(K\), proving \eqref{eq:amplitude-zero-count} and
\eqref{eq:W-local-zero}.

By \eqref{eq:W-on-g}, the endpoint exponent of \(W\) is the order of
\(B(\gamma)\) minus that of \(\gamma^rg'(\gamma)\).
At a finite endpoint, \(z_e-g(t)\) vanishes to order \(k\) at \(t_e\), so
\(t^rg'(t)\) vanishes there to order \(k-1\).  By
\Cref{lem:principal-endpoint-regularity},
\(\gamma-t_e=\gamma_e\delta(1+O(\delta))\), and the asserted exponent
\(p=\nu-(k-1)\) follows.  At the upper endpoint for \(r>1\),
\(\gamma=\eta T(\eta)\), \(B(\gamma)=B(0)(1+O(\eta))\), and
\[
 \partial_tD(\gamma,z)
 =Q'(\gamma)-\frac{rQ(\gamma)}{\gamma}
 =-\frac{rQ(0)}{\gamma}(1+O(\gamma)),
\]
so \(W=\eta V(\eta)\) with \(V(0)\ne0\).

Finally, near an interior zero,
\[
 \frac{W'}{W}
 =\frac{\nu_j}{\theta-\theta_j}+O(1),
\]
and near an endpoint,
\[
 \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\delta}\log W=\frac p\delta+O(1).
\]
The singular terms are real on each side, hence disappear from
\(\Im(W'/W)=\psi'\).  Compactness of the remaining subarcs gives
\eqref{eq:phase-derivative-bound}.
\end{proof}

The singular parts of \(W'/W\) are real: amplitude zeros and endpoint powers
change the size of \(W\), but introduce no singular term into
\(\psi'=\Im(W'/W)\).

The resulting phase variation can be bounded uniformly over all numerator
weights of a given degree; the geometric input is Radon's viewing-angle bound.

\begin{lemma}[Radon's viewing-angle bound]\label{lem:viewing-angle}
Let \(\gamma:[s_0,s_1]\to\C\), \(s\mapsto\gamma(s)\), be an injective regular
real-analytic arc, real-analytic on a neighborhood of the closed interval.  Choose
a continuous tangent angle \(\vartheta=\arg\gamma'\) and put
\[
 \mathcal K_\gamma=\operatorname{Var}_{[s_0,s_1]}\vartheta.
\]
Then for every \(\beta\in\C\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:viewing-angle-bound}
 \sum_{\mathcal I}\operatorname{Var}_{\mathcal I}\arg(\gamma-\beta)
 \le \mathcal K_\gamma+\pi,
\end{equation}
where the sum is over the components \(\mathcal I\) of
\([s_0,s_1]\setminus\gamma^{-1}(\{\beta\})\), and a continuous branch of the
argument is used on each component.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
This is Radon's theorem on the viewing angle of an arc of bounded
rotation, together with the identification of total rotation with tangent-angle
variation; see \cite[Lems.~4.39--4.40]{Fillastre2023Reshetnyak}.  For
\(\beta\notin\gamma([s_0,s_1])\), the viewing angle is a continuous argument of
\(\gamma-\beta\), and Radon's bound is exactly
\(\mathcal K_\gamma+\pi\).  If \(\beta\) lies on the arc, injectivity gives a unique
preimage.  Radon's one-sided viewing angle at that point gives continuous
argument branches on the complementary components, and its variation over
\([s_0,s_1]\) bounds the sum in \eqref{eq:viewing-angle-bound}.  Since the
total rotation of a regular arc is the variation of its tangent angle, the same
bound follows.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:linear-phase-variation}
There are constants \(\kappa_0,\kappa_1<\infty\), depending only on the
denominator, such that for every \(B\in\R[t]\) with \(B(0)\ne0\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:linear-phase-variation}
 \sum_{\mathcal I}\operatorname{Var}_{\mathcal I}\arg W
 \le \kappa_0+\kappa_1\deg B,
\end{equation}
where the sum is over the components \(\mathcal I\) of
\((0,\pi/r)\setminus\{W=0\}\).  One may take
\(\kappa_1=\mathcal K_\gamma+\pi\), where \(\mathcal K_\gamma\) is the total tangent-angle
variation of the principal arc.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By \Cref{lem:principal-endpoint-regularity}, the principal arc
\(\gamma(\theta)=t_+(\theta)\) is a regular real-analytic arc on the closed
parameter interval.  Factor over \(\C\),
\[
 B(t)=b\prod_{\ell=1}^{K}(t-\beta_\ell),
 \qquad K=\deg B.
\]
By \Cref{lem:amplitude-divisor}, deleting the zeros of \(W\) is exactly deleting
the parameters where \(B\circ\gamma\) vanishes.  On each remaining component,
choose continuous arguments for the factors of \(B\).  Then
\[
 \arg B(\gamma(\theta))
 =\arg b+\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\arg\bigl(\gamma(\theta)-\beta_\ell\bigr)
\]
up to an additive constant, so its variation is at most the sum of the factor
variations.  For fixed \(\ell\), the remaining components refine those of
\([0,\pi/r]\setminus\gamma^{-1}(\{\beta_\ell\})\); hence their summed variation
is bounded by the left side of \eqref{eq:viewing-angle-bound}.  Thus
\Cref{lem:viewing-angle} bounds the \(\ell\)th contribution by
\(\mathcal K_\gamma+\pi\), and summing over \(\ell\) gives
\(K(\mathcal K_\gamma+\pi)\).  The remaining factor in
\eqref{eq:W-on-g},
\[
 \bigl(\gamma(\theta)^rg'(\gamma(\theta))\bigr)^{-1},
\]
depends only on \(Q,r\).  It has finite total phase variation: it is
real-analytic and nonvanishing in the open interval, and by
\Cref{lem:principal-endpoint-regularity} together with
\eqref{eq:W-endpoint-form} for \(B=1\) it differs near each endpoint from a
real-analytic nonvanishing function by the positive factor \(\delta^p\) alone,
which contributes nothing to the argument.  A continuous branch of that
argument is therefore real-analytic on the closed interval, hence of finite
variation.  Adding this fixed variation proves
\eqref{eq:linear-phase-variation}.
\end{proof}

\section{Principal-pair dominance and the fixed-numerator theorem}\label{sec:dominance}

\subsection{Weighted principal-pair dominance}\label{subsec:weighted-dominance}

We now extend the denominator-only estimates of
\cite[Prop.~3]{Forgacs2017RationalDenominator} to the fixed polynomial weight
\(B\).  The proof has four pieces.  On compact interior subarcs the principal
pair has a fixed modulus gap from every other pole.  Near an endpoint that gap
may close linearly, but outside an angular window of width \(O(M^{-1})\) this
still gives fixed exponential suppression.  Near a zero of the principal
amplitude, an exponentially small deleted interval restores dominance.  Every
pole outside the retained endpoint cluster is handled at once by the contour
remainder.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:near-cluster-suppression}
Let \(c,\varepsilon,C_W>0\), let \(M\ge0\) be an integer, and let
\(\delta\in(0,\varepsilon]\).  Suppose \(W(\delta)\ne0\) and that finitely many
pairs \(W_j(\delta),\zeta_j(\delta)\in\C\) satisfy
\[
 |W_j(\delta)|\le C_W|W(\delta)|,
 \qquad
 |\zeta_j(\delta)|\ge1+c\delta.
\]
Then, with \(c'=c/(1+c\varepsilon)\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:near-cluster-suppression}
 \frac1{|W(\delta)|}
 \left|\sum_jW_j(\delta)\zeta_j(\delta)^{-M-1}\right|
 \le C_W\,\#\{j\}\,e^{-c'M\delta}.
\end{equation}
In particular, for \(h>0\) the right side is at most
\(C_W\#\{j\}e^{-c'h}\) whenever \(M\delta\ge h\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The inequality \(\log(1+x)\ge x/(1+x)\) for \(x\ge0\) gives
\[
 |\zeta_j|^{-M-1}
 \le(1+c\delta)^{-M-1}
 \le e^{-c'M\delta}.
\]
Summing the residue bounds proves \eqref{eq:near-cluster-suppression}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Weighted principal-pair dominance]\label{thm:weighted-dominance}
There is a constant \(h=h(Q,r)>0\) with the following property.  For every
fixed \(B\), there are \(M_0=M_0(Q,r,B)\) and \(c=c(Q,r,B)>0\).  For
\(M\ge M_0\), define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:amplitude-deletion}
 \Theta_{j,M}=\left\{\theta:
 |\theta-\theta_j|<e^{-cM/\nu_j}\right\}
\end{equation}
for the zeros in \Cref{lem:amplitude-divisor}.  Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dominance-bound}
 |R_M(\theta)|\le\frac12|W(\theta)|
\end{equation}
whenever
\begin{equation}\label{eq:retained-range}
 \frac hM\le\theta\le\frac\pi r-\frac hM,
 \qquad
 \theta\notin\bigcup_{j=1}^J \Theta_{j,M}.
\end{equation}
The total length of the deleted amplitude intervals is exponentially small.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix first a denominator-only endpoint radius
\(\varepsilon_*\in(0,\pi/(2r))\) on which the endpoint expansions and the
linear modulus-gap estimates of \Cref{thm:FT-geometry} hold.  For the fixed
weight \(B\), decrease to some \(0<\varepsilon\le\varepsilon_*\) so that every
zero \(\theta_j\) of \Cref{lem:amplitude-divisor} lies in
\((\varepsilon,\pi/r-\varepsilon)\); then \(W\) has no zero in
\((0,\varepsilon]\) or in \([\pi/r-\varepsilon,\pi/r)\).  We use
\Cref{lem:contour-separation} with only the roots whose modulus can compete
with the principal pair retained inside the contour.  On the compact interior,
the uniform minimum-modulus gap and a finite cover give fixed local contours
containing only the principal pair.  Near the lower endpoint a fixed circle
contains the principal pair when the smallest zero is simple and the full
\(\rho\)-root endpoint cluster when it has multiplicity \(\rho>1\).  Near the
upper endpoint it contains the principal pair for \(r=1\) and the \(r\)-root
small cluster for \(r>1\).  The fixed-gap assertions of
\Cref{thm:FT-geometry} separate those clusters from every remaining finite
root; roots that leave every bounded set remain outside the same fixed circle.

After decreasing \(\varepsilon\), the hypotheses of
\eqref{eq:contour-remainder-bound} are therefore uniform on each of these
regions.  At the unbounded upper endpoint \(r>1\), if the separating circle is
\(|t|=R\), then \(z(\theta)R^r\) dominates \(Q(t)\) uniformly on the circle
for \(\theta\) sufficiently close to \(\pi/r\), so the required bound on
\(B/D\) is uniform there as well.  In particular, after normalization by
\(\tau^{M+1}\), every contour remainder is \(O(\sigma^M)\) for some
region-dependent \(\sigma<1\).  We shall
also use repeatedly that at any nonzero denominator root
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Dprime-identity}
 \partial_tD(t,z)=Q'(t)-\frac{rQ(t)}t.
\end{equation}

\emph{Compact interior.}
On \(\theta\in[\varepsilon,\pi/r-\varepsilon]\), the retained cluster is the principal pair alone, so
\Cref{lem:contour-separation} and \eqref{eq:principal-decomposition} give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:interior-remainder}
 |R_M(\theta)|\le C_I\sigma_I^M,
 \qquad \varepsilon\le\theta\le\pi/r-\varepsilon,
\end{equation}
for some \(C_I<\infty\) and \(\sigma_I<1\).  Choose
\[
 c=\tfrac12\log\frac1{\sigma_I}>0.
\]
Choose pairwise disjoint fixed neighborhoods of the finitely many
\(\theta_j\).  By \eqref{eq:W-local-zero}, after increasing \(M_0\) so that every
\(\Theta_{j,M}\) lies in its fixed neighborhood, there is a \(C_A>0\) such that
throughout the retained compact interior,
\[
 |W(\theta)|\ge C_Ae^{-cM}.
\]
The factor \(1/\nu_j\) in the deletion radius is what makes this lower bound
independent of the multiplicity of the amplitude zero.  Indeed, in the
neighborhood of \(\theta_j\) one has
\(|W(\theta)|\ge C_j|\theta-\theta_j|^{\nu_j}\), while away from all those
neighborhoods \(|W|\) has a positive minimum.  Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:interior-relative-remainder}
 \frac{|R_M(\theta)|}{|W(\theta)|}
 \le \frac{C_I}{C_A}\sigma_I^{M/2}=o(1)
\end{equation}
uniformly on the retained compact interior.

For the endpoint regions it is useful to record once the comparison between a
fixed-contour error and the principal amplitude.  If \(\delta\) is the angular
distance to the endpoint, \Cref{lem:amplitude-divisor} gives
\(|W(\theta)|\asymp\delta^p\) for a fixed integer \(p\).  Thus any normalized
contour error \(\mathcal E_M(\theta)\) satisfying
\(|\mathcal E_M(\theta)|\le C\sigma^M\) also satisfies, for every fixed \(h>0\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:endpoint-contour-relative-bound}
 \frac{|\mathcal E_M(\theta)|}{|W(\theta)|}
 \le C_h\sigma^M M^{\max\{p,0\}}=o(1)
 \qquad(\delta\ge h/M),
\end{equation}
uniformly in the endpoint region.

\emph{The lower endpoint, \(\theta\downarrow0\).}
If the smallest zero of \(Q\) is simple, the retained endpoint cluster is the
principal pair alone.  Hence \(R_M\) is just the contour error and
\eqref{eq:endpoint-contour-relative-bound} gives
\[
 \frac{|R_M(\theta)|}{|W(\theta)|}=o(1)
 \qquad(h/M\le\theta\le\varepsilon).
\]
This includes without distinction any parameter at which a nonprincipal root
escapes to infinity.

Now let the smallest zero \(x_1\) have multiplicity \(\rho>1\).  The endpoint
analysis of \cite[Prop.~3]{Forgacs2017RationalDenominator} produces a cluster
of actual roots \(t_j(\theta)\to x_1\) with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lower-cluster-expansion}
 t_j(\theta)=x_1+\alpha_j\theta+O(\theta^2),
 \qquad
 \alpha_j=-\frac{x_1\omega_j}{\sin(\pi/\rho)},
 \qquad
 \omega_j=e^{(2j-1)\pi i/\rho}.
\end{equation}
The principal upper branch is the index with \(\omega_+=e^{-i\pi/\rho}\), for
which \(t_+=\tau e^{i\theta}\); write \(\alpha_+\) for its coefficient.  The
\(\alpha_j\) are distinct and nonzero, so all members of this cluster are
simple and pairwise distinct for sufficiently small \(\theta>0\).  Also
\(|\alpha_j|=x_1/\sin(\pi/\rho)\) for every \(j\).  Together with
\(\tau(\theta)=x_1(1-\cot(\pi/\rho)\theta)+O(\theta^2)\) this gives
\(|\zeta_\pm(\theta)|=1\) for the principal pair and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lower-cluster-gap}
 |\zeta_j(\theta)|\ge1+c_0\theta
\end{equation}
for every other cluster member, after decreasing \(\varepsilon\) if necessary.

Let \(\nu=\operatorname{ord}_{x_1}B\ge0\), and for a cluster member put
\[
 W_j(\theta)=\mathcal W(t_j(\theta),z(\theta)).
\]
Thus \(W_+=W\).  From \eqref{eq:lower-cluster-expansion},
\[
 B(t_j)=\frac{B^{(\nu)}(x_1)}{\nu!}\,
 \alpha_j^\nu\theta^\nu(1+O(\theta)).
\]
By \eqref{eq:Dprime-identity} and Taylor expansion of \(Q\) at \(x_1\),
\[
 Q'(t_j)-\frac{rQ(t_j)}{t_j}
 =\frac{Q^{(\rho)}(x_1)}{(\rho-1)!}\,
 \alpha_j^{\rho-1}\theta^{\rho-1}(1+O(\theta)).
\]
Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lower-residue-ratio}
 \frac{W_j(\theta)}{W(\theta)}
 =\left(\frac{\alpha_j}{\alpha_+}\right)^{\nu-\rho+1}
  (1+O(\theta)),
 \qquad
 \left|\frac{W_j(\theta)}{W(\theta)}\right|\longrightarrow1.
\end{equation}
The error is uniform over the finite cluster, so after decreasing
\(\varepsilon\) we may assume \(|W_j|\le2|W|\) for every member.

The contour now retains the full \(\rho\)-root cluster.  Thus
\Cref{lem:contour-separation} gives
\[
 R_M(\theta)
 =\sum_{j\ne+,-}W_j(\theta)\zeta_j(\theta)^{-M-1}
  +\mathcal E_M(\theta),
 \qquad
 |\mathcal E_M(\theta)|\le C_L\sigma_L^M.
\]
Applying \Cref{lem:near-cluster-suppression} with
\(\delta=\theta\), \(C_W=2\), and \(c=c_0\), put
\(c_0'=c_0/(1+c_0\varepsilon)\).  On \(\theta\ge h/M\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lower-cluster-relative-bound}
 \frac1{|W(\theta)|}
 \left|\sum_{j\ne+,-}W_j(\theta)\zeta_j(\theta)^{-M-1}\right|
 \le2(\rho-2)e^{-c_0'h}.
\end{equation}
For \(\rho=2\) the sum is absent, while
\eqref{eq:endpoint-contour-relative-bound} makes
\(|\mathcal E_M|/|W|=o(1)\).

\emph{The upper endpoint, \(\theta\uparrow\pi/r\).}
When \(r=1\), the retained endpoint cluster is again the principal pair alone,
so \eqref{eq:endpoint-contour-relative-bound}, with
\(\delta=\pi-\theta\), gives
\[
 \frac{|R_M(\theta)|}{|W(\theta)|}=o(1)
 \qquad(h/M\le\pi-\theta\le\varepsilon).
\]
A zero of \(B\) at the collision point is already included in the endpoint
exponent \(p\).

Assume \(r>1\) and set \(\eta=\pi/r-\theta\).  By
\Cref{thm:FT-geometry}, the retained small cluster approaches the \(r\)th roots
of \(-1\), the principal roots approaching \(e^{\pm i\pi/r}\), and every other
member satisfies
\[
 |\zeta_j(\theta)|\ge1+c_1\eta
\]
for some \(c_1>0\).  The limiting normalized roots are distinct, so the small
cluster is simple for sufficiently small \(\eta>0\).  Using
\eqref{eq:Dprime-identity}, \(t_j=\tau\zeta_j\), and \(B(0)\ne0\),
\[
 \partial_tD(t_j,z(\theta))
 =-\frac{rQ(0)}{\tau\zeta_j}(1+O(\tau)),
\]
so with \(W_j=\mathcal W(t_j,z(\theta))\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:upper-residue-ratio}
 W_j(\theta)
 =\frac{B(0)}{rQ(0)}\tau\zeta_j(1+O(\tau)),
 \qquad
 \frac{W_j(\theta)}{W(\theta)}
 =\frac{\zeta_j(\theta)}{\zeta_+(\theta)}(1+O(\tau)).
\end{equation}
Hence \(|W_j/W|\to1\) uniformly over the finite cluster; after decreasing
\(\varepsilon\), use \(|W_j|\le2|W|\).  The contour-separated expansion gives
\[
 R_M(\theta)
 =\sum_{j\ne+,-}W_j(\theta)\zeta_j(\theta)^{-M-1}
  +\mathcal E_M(\theta),
 \qquad
 |\mathcal E_M(\theta)|\le C_U\sigma_U^M.
\]
Applying \Cref{lem:near-cluster-suppression} with
\(\delta=\eta\), \(C_W=2\), and \(c=c_1\), put
\(c_1'=c_1/(1+c_1\varepsilon)\).  On \(\eta\ge h/M\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:upper-cluster-relative-bound}
 \frac1{|W(\theta)|}
 \left|\sum_{j\ne+,-}W_j(\theta)\zeta_j(\theta)^{-M-1}\right|
 \le2(r-2)e^{-c_1'h}.
\end{equation}
For \(r=2\) the sum is absent, and
\eqref{eq:endpoint-contour-relative-bound} again gives
\(|\mathcal E_M|/|W|=o(1)\).

It remains only to choose the constants, in an order that keeps \(h\) free of
\(B\): first the endpoint rates, then \(h\), then the \(B\)-dependent
threshold \(M_0\).  Because
\(\varepsilon\le\varepsilon_*\),
\[
 c_0'\ge\frac{c_0}{1+c_0\varepsilon_*},
 \qquad
 c_1'\ge\frac{c_1}{1+c_1\varepsilon_*}
\]
whenever the corresponding endpoint cluster is present.  These lower bounds
depend only on \(Q,r\).  Choose \(h=h(Q,r)\) so large that
\[
 2(\rho-2)e^{-c_0'h}\le\frac14
 \quad(\rho>1),
 \qquad
 2(r-2)e^{-c_1'h}\le\frac14
 \quad(r>1),
\]
which is possible using those denominator-only lower bounds.  Then choose
\(M_0\) sufficiently large that \(h/M_0<\varepsilon\), that
\eqref{eq:interior-relative-remainder} is at most \(1/2\), and that every
endpoint contour error in \eqref{eq:endpoint-contour-relative-bound} is at
most \(1/4\).  Combining the explicit near-cluster terms with the contour
remainders gives \eqref{eq:dominance-bound} throughout
\eqref{eq:retained-range}.  Finally,
\[
 \sum_{j=1}^J |\Theta_{j,M}|
 \le2\sum_{j=1}^J e^{-cM/\nu_j},
\]
which is exponentially small because the number and multiplicities of the
amplitude zeros are fixed.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:endpoint-sign-free}
The proof does not require the exact sign of the full weighted endpoint
cluster.  Outside angular windows of width \(h/M\), the linear endpoint modulus
gap becomes the fixed exponential suppression \(e^{-c_0'h}\) at the lower
endpoint and \(e^{-c_1'h}\) at the upper.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Angular discrepancy and proof of the main theorem}\label{subsec:proof}

The dominance theorem gives more than a global bounded defect: the zeros have
uniformly bounded discrepancy in the angular coordinate.  The resulting
quantitative statement will imply both \Cref{thm:main} and the limiting measure
in \Cref{prop:equidistribution}.

For \(0\le\alpha<\beta\le\pi/r\), put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:angular-subinterval}
 I_{\alpha,\beta}
 =\{z(\theta):\alpha<\theta<\beta\}\subset I_{Q,r},
\end{equation}
and let \(Z_M(\alpha,\beta)\) denote the number of zeros of \(F_M\) in
\(I_{\alpha,\beta}\), counted with multiplicity.
Here ``angular'' refers to the Forg\'acs--Tran spectral parameter \(\theta\),
not to the classical Erd\H{o}s--Tur\'an discrepancy of the complex arguments of
polynomial zeros \cite[Thm.~I]{ErdosTuran1950Roots}.

\begin{proposition}[Uniform angular zero discrepancy]
\label{prop:angular-discrepancy}
There are constants \(C_0=C_0(Q,r)\) and \(C_1=C_1(Q,r)\) such that, for every
\(B\in\R[t]\) with \(B(0)\ne0\), writing \(K=\deg B\), all sufficiently large
\(M\) satisfy, uniformly for \(0\le\alpha<\beta\le\pi/r\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:angular-distinct-lower}
 \#_{\mathrm{dist}}\{x\in I_{\alpha,\beta}:F_M(x)=0\}
 \ge
 \frac{M+1}{\pi}(\beta-\alpha)-C_0-C_1K,
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:angular-discrepancy}
 \left|
 Z_M(\alpha,\beta)
 -\frac{M+1}{\pi}(\beta-\alpha)
 \right|
 \le C_0+C_1K.
\end{equation}
The threshold in \(M\) may depend on \(B\), but the discrepancy constants do
not.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let \(h=h(Q,r)\), \(c\), and \(M_0\) be as in
\Cref{thm:weighted-dominance}, and define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Omega-M}
 \Omega_M=
 \left[\frac hM,\frac\pi r-\frac hM\right]
 \setminus\bigcup_{j=1}^J\Theta_{j,M}.
\end{equation}
By \eqref{eq:amplitude-zero-count}, \(J\le K\).  Since the amplitude windows
are exponentially small, after increasing the \(B\)-dependent threshold we
may assume
\begin{equation}\label{eq:amplitude-window-negligible}
 (M+1)\sum_{j=1}^J|\Theta_{j,M}|\le1.
\end{equation}

Fix \(\alpha<\beta\).  On each component of
\(\Omega_M\cap(\alpha,\beta)\), choose a continuous branch
\(\psi=\arg W\) and put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Phi-def}
 \Phi_M(\theta)=(M+1)\theta-\psi(\theta).
\end{equation}
By \eqref{eq:phase-derivative-bound}, \(\Phi_M'>0\) for all sufficiently
large \(M\).  At a point where \(\Phi_M=k\pi\), the principal term in
\eqref{eq:principal-decomposition} has sign \((-1)^k\), and
\Cref{thm:weighted-dominance} gives the same sign for the complete
coefficient.  Consecutive phase points therefore enclose distinct zeros.

On a component \(\mathcal I\) of \(\Omega_M\cap(\alpha,\beta)\), the increase
of \(\Phi_M\) is at least
\((M+1)|\mathcal I|-\operatorname{Var}_{\mathcal I}\psi\), so \(\Phi_M\) meets
\(\pi\mathbb Z\) at least
\(\bigl((M+1)|\mathcal I|-\operatorname{Var}_{\mathcal I}\psi\bigr)/\pi-1\)
times, and \(\mathcal I\) carries at least one zero fewer.
The number of components is at most \(J+1\), so summing the phase count gives
\begin{align*}
 \#_{\mathrm{dist}}\{x\in I_{\alpha,\beta}:F_M(x)=0\}
 &\ge
 \frac{M+1}{\pi}
 |\Omega_M\cap(\alpha,\beta)|
 -\frac1\pi\sum_{\mathcal I}\operatorname{Var}_{\mathcal I}\psi
 -2(J+1).
\end{align*}
The length deleted from \((\alpha,\beta)\) is at most
\(2h/M+\sum_j|\Theta_{j,M}|\).  Using
\eqref{eq:amplitude-window-negligible},
\Cref{cor:linear-phase-variation}, and \(J\le K\), we obtain
\eqref{eq:angular-distinct-lower} with constants depending only on \(Q,r\).

For the upper bound, apply \eqref{eq:angular-distinct-lower} to the two
complementary angular intervals \((0,\alpha)\) and
\((\beta,\pi/r)\), omitting either interval when it is empty.  Together they contain at least
\[
 \frac{M+1}{\pi}
 \left(\frac\pi r-(\beta-\alpha)\right)
 -2(C_0+C_1K)
\]
distinct zeros outside \(I_{\alpha,\beta}\), after enlarging the constants
once.  Since \(\deg F_M=\lfloor M/r\rfloor\) eventually,
\[
 Z_M(\alpha,\beta)
 \le
 \left\lfloor\frac Mr\right\rfloor
 -\frac{M+1}{\pi}
 \left(\frac\pi r-(\beta-\alpha)\right)
 +2(C_0+C_1K)
 \le
 \frac{M+1}{\pi}(\beta-\alpha)+2(C_0+C_1K).
\]
The distinct-zero lower bound also gives the corresponding lower bound for
\(Z_M\).  Renaming the constants proves
\eqref{eq:angular-discrepancy}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \Cref{thm:main}]
Let
\[
 L_N(t)=t^{\lambda_N}B_N(t)
\]
be the canonical Laurent restriction from \Cref{lem:laurent-reduction}.  For
all sufficiently large \(m\),
\[
 P_m(z)=F_{m-\lambda_N}(z)
\]
with the fixed weight \(B_N\).  Apply
\Cref{prop:angular-discrepancy} to the full angular interval
\((0,\pi/r)\).  Together with \Cref{lem:eventual-degree}, it gives, for all
sufficiently large \(m\),
\[
 \#_{\mathrm{dist}}\{x\in I_{Q,r}:P_m(x)=0\}
 \ge \deg P_m-O_{Q,r,N}(1),
\]
and hence only \(O_{Q,r,N}(1)\) remaining zeros counted with multiplicity.
More precisely, the constants in
\eqref{eq:angular-distinct-lower} depend on the weight only through
\(\deg B_N\), so after increasing the denominator-only constant by one, the
eventual defect is at most
\(C_0+C_1\deg B_N\).  Since
\(I_{Q,r}\subset(0,\infty)\), the same qualitative bound holds outside the
positive ray.  The exact eventual degree formula is
\eqref{eq:exact-eventual-degree-shift}.  Enlarging the all-index constant to
cover the finitely many preceding nonzero coefficient polynomials proves the
theorem.
\end{proof}



\section{Global and local zero laws}\label{sec:consequences}

\subsection{Global zero distribution and angular rigidity}\label{subsec:zero-bulk}

After normalization by degree, the zeros of every fixed numerator converge
weakly to a common limiting measure determined by the denominator.  For
\(P\in\R[z]\) of positive degree \(k\), with zeros
\(\xi_1,\dots,\xi_k\) repeated by multiplicity, write
\[
 \mu[P]=\frac1k\sum_{i=1}^k\delta_{\xi_i}.
\]

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:equidistribution}
Let \(z:(0,\pi/r)\to I_{Q,r}\) be the parameterization of
\Cref{thm:FT-geometry}, let \(\mu_\infty\) be the pushforward of
\(\tfrac r\pi\dd\theta\) under \(z\), and write
\[
 N\!\left(t,-\frac{Q(t)}{t^r}\right)=t^{\lambda_N}B_N(t),
 \qquad B_N(0)\ne0,
\]
for the canonical Laurent restriction.  There are constants
\(D_0=D_0(Q,r)\) and \(D_1=D_1(Q,r)\) such that, for all sufficiently large
\(m\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normalized-angular-discrepancy}
 \sup_{0\le\alpha<\beta\le\pi/r}
 \left|
  \mu[P_m](I_{\alpha,\beta})
  -\frac r\pi(\beta-\alpha)
 \right|
 \le
 \frac{D_0+D_1\deg B_N}{\deg P_m}.
\end{equation}
Consequently \(\mu[P_m]\Rightarrow\mu_\infty\) along the coefficient
polynomials of positive degree.  In particular, every fixed numerator has the
same limiting zero distribution as the denominator-only sequence \(H_m\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Put \(M=m-\lambda_N\), so that for all sufficiently large \(m\),
\(P_m=F_M\) with weight \(B_N\), and put
\(d_M=\deg P_m=\lfloor M/r\rfloor\).  By
\Cref{prop:angular-discrepancy}, with \(K=\deg B_N\),
\[
 \left|
  Z_M(\alpha,\beta)
  -\frac{M+1}{\pi}(\beta-\alpha)
 \right|
 \le C_0+C_1K.
\]
Write \(M=rd_M+s\) with \(0\le s<r\).  Since
\(0\le\beta-\alpha\le\pi/r\),
\[
 \frac{\beta-\alpha}{\pi}
 \left|\frac{M+1}{d_M}-r\right|
 =\frac{\beta-\alpha}{\pi}\frac{s+1}{d_M}
 \le\frac1{d_M}.
\]
Dividing the discrepancy estimate by \(d_M\) therefore proves
\eqref{eq:normalized-angular-discrepancy}, after increasing the
 denominator-only constant by one.

For weak convergence, let \(0<\alpha<\beta<\pi/r\).  By
\eqref{eq:normalized-angular-discrepancy} the compact interval
\(z([\alpha,\beta])\) carries \(\mu[P_m]\)-mass at least
\(\frac r\pi(\beta-\alpha)-(D_0+D_1\deg B_N)/\deg P_m\), which exceeds any
prescribed bound below \(1\) once \(\beta-\alpha\) is close enough to
\(\pi/r\) and \(m\) is large; the sequence is therefore tight.

The same estimate gives
\(\mu[P_m](I_{\alpha,\beta})\to\mu_\infty(I_{\alpha,\beta})\) for every such
\(\alpha,\beta\), and \(\mu_\infty\) has no
atoms because \(z\) is strictly monotone, so these intervals form a
convergence-determining class.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:angular-rigidity}
For all sufficiently large \(m\), list the zeros of \(P_m\) in \(I_{Q,r}\),
counted with multiplicity, as
\[
 x_{1,m}\le\cdots\le x_{s_m,m},
 \qquad
 \theta_{j,m}=z^{-1}(x_{j,m}),
\]
and put \(M=m-\lambda_N\).  There are constants
\(E_0=E_0(Q,r)\), \(E_1=E_1(Q,r)\) such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:angular-clock}
 \left|
  \theta_{j,m}-\frac{\pi j}{M+1}
 \right|
 \le
 \frac{\pi(E_0+E_1\deg B_N)}{M+1}
 \qquad(1\le j\le s_m).
\end{equation}
Thus every bulk-zero angle lies within \(O_{Q,r,N}(m^{-1})\) of the uniform
angular clock.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Let \(\Delta_B=C_0+C_1\deg B_N\) be the discrepancy constant in
\Cref{prop:angular-discrepancy}.  Fix a zero angle \(\theta\), and let
\(j_-\) and \(j_+\) be respectively the numbers of interval zeros with angle
strictly less than, and at most, \(\theta\).  Applying
\eqref{eq:angular-discrepancy} to \((0,\theta)\), and then to
\((0,\theta+\varepsilon)\) followed by \(\varepsilon\downarrow0\), gives
\[
 \left|j_--\frac{M+1}{\pi}\theta\right|\le \Delta_B,
 \qquad
 \left|j_+-\frac{M+1}{\pi}\theta\right|\le \Delta_B.
\]
Every index \(j\) belonging to that multiple zero satisfies
\(j_-<j\le j_+\), hence
\[
 \left|j-\frac{M+1}{\pi}\theta\right|\le \Delta_B+1.
\]
Absorb the final \(1\) into the denominator-only constant and rearrange.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Local phase quantization and strong-clock spacing}\label{subsec:strong-clock}

The global localization in \Cref{cor:angular-rigidity} is reminiscent of clock
behavior for orthogonal-polynomial zeros \cite[p.~488]{LastSimon2008Clock}, but by itself
it is weaker than strong-clock spacing: the error in \eqref{eq:angular-clock} is
of the same order \(M^{-1}\) as the nominal spacing.  Exponentially accurate
finite-size zero-location laws from two-term spectral competition also appear
in a different setting in \cite{Biskup2004PartitionZeros}.  On zero-free compact
subarcs here, the phase law is much more rigid.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:local-strong-clock}
Fix \(B\in\R[t]\) with \(B(0)\ne0\), put
\[
 F_M(z)=[t^M]\frac{B(t)}{Q(t)+zt^r},
\]
and let \(W\) be the principal amplitude \eqref{eq:W-def}.  Let \(\mathcal J\)
be a compact interval contained in one component of the complement in
\((0,\pi/r)\) of the amplitude zeros of \Cref{lem:amplitude-divisor}, and
choose a continuous phase \(\psi=\arg W\) there.  Then there are \(\sigma\in(0,1)\) and \(M_0\) such
that, for \(M\ge M_0\), every zero of \(F_M\) in \(z(\mathcal J)\) is simple
and is \(z(\theta_{k,M})\) for a unique \(\theta_{k,M}\in\mathcal J\), the
indices \(k\) running consecutively, with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:local-phase-quantization}
 (M+1)\theta_{k,M}-\psi(\theta_{k,M})
 =\left(k+\tfrac12\right)\pi+O(\sigma^M).
\end{equation}
For consecutive such zero angles in \(\mathcal J\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:local-strong-clock}
 \theta_{k+1,M}-\theta_{k,M}
 =\frac{\pi}{M+1}
  +\frac{\pi\psi'(\theta_{k,M})}{(M+1)^2}
  +O_{Q,r,B,\mathcal J}(M^{-3}).
\end{equation}
In particular the zeros have local strong-clock spacing after unfolding by the
Forg\'acs--Tran parameter \(\theta\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Choose a compact interval \(\mathcal J_0\) in the same zero-free component with
\(\mathcal J\Subset\operatorname{int}\mathcal J_0\), extend \(\psi\)
continuously to \(\mathcal J_0\), and write
\[
 G_M(\theta)=\tau(\theta)^{M+1}F_M(z(\theta)),
 \qquad
 \Phi_M(\theta)=(M+1)\theta-\psi(\theta).
\]
On \(\mathcal J_0\), the compact-interior contour expansion gives
\[
 G_M(\theta)=2|W(\theta)|\cos\Phi_M(\theta)+R_M(\theta).
\]
By the compact-uniform separation of \Cref{thm:FT-geometry}, a finite cover of
\(\mathcal J_0\) by parameter pieces carries fixed circles, each enclosing the
principal pair alone and each keeping \(D\) away from zero for all \(z\) in a
complex neighborhood of the corresponding piece of \(z(\mathcal J_0)\).  Over
such a neighborhood \Cref{lem:contour-separation} identifies \(R_M\) with the
normalized contour remainder \(\tau^{M+1}E_M\), bounds it by a constant times
\(\sigma^M\) for some \(\sigma<1\), and makes \(E_M\) holomorphic in \(z\);
Cauchy's estimate on a fixed disk then bounds \(\partial_zE_M\) by the same
exponential.  Since \(z\) and \(\tau\) are real-analytic on \(\mathcal J_0\)
and differentiating \(\tau^{M+1}\) contributes at most one factor of \(M\),
this gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:C1-interior-remainder}
 \|R_M\|_{C^1(\mathcal J_0)}=O(M\sigma^M).
\end{equation}

Since \(|W|\) is bounded away from zero on \(\mathcal J_0\) and \(\psi'\) is
bounded by \eqref{eq:phase-derivative-bound},
\[
 \Phi_M'=M+1-\psi'=M+O(1)>0,
\]
so \(\Phi_M\) is a diffeomorphism of \(\mathcal J_0\) onto its image.  Take
\(u=\Phi_M(\theta)\) as coordinate and read \(G_M/(2|W|)\) as a function of
\(u\).  Then
\[
 \frac{G_M}{2|W|}=\cos u+\varepsilon_M(u),
 \qquad
 \|\varepsilon_M\|_{C^1}=O(\sigma^M),
\]
because \(\mathrm du/\mathrm d\theta\asymp M\), so the factor \(M\) in
\eqref{eq:C1-interior-remainder} is exactly absorbed by the change of variable.
Fix \(\delta\in(0,\pi/4)\).  On \(|u-(k+\tfrac12)\pi|\le\delta\) the derivative
of \(\cos u+\varepsilon_M\) is bounded away from zero, and the function changes
sign across the interval; outside those neighborhoods \(|\cos u|\ge\sin\delta\)
exceeds \(\|\varepsilon_M\|_\infty\) for large \(M\).  Hence every such
neighborhood contained in \(\Phi_M(\mathcal J_0)\) carries exactly one zero, and
that zero is simple.  Every zero of \(G_M\) whose angle lies in \(\mathcal J\)
belongs to one of them, and there is no other zero on \(\mathcal J\); since
\(\mathcal J\) is an interval and \(\Phi_M\) is increasing, the corresponding
indices form a consecutive block.  This proves
\eqref{eq:local-phase-quantization}.
Finally \(z'(\theta)=g'(\gamma(\theta))\gamma'(\theta)\ne0\) by
\eqref{eq:principal-simple} and \Cref{lem:principal-endpoint-regularity},
and \(\tau>0\); differentiating
\(G_M=\tau^{M+1}F_M\circ z\) at a zero of \(F_M\) therefore shows that
simplicity in \(u\) or \(\theta\) is simplicity of the corresponding zero of
the polynomial \(F_M\).

For consecutive zero angles put
\(\Delta_M=\theta_{k+1,M}-\theta_{k,M}\).  The phase quantization gives
\[
 \pi+O(\sigma^M)
 =(M+1)\Delta_M
  -\bigl(\psi(\theta_{k+1,M})-\psi(\theta_{k,M})\bigr).
\]
Since \(\Phi_M'\asymp M\), first \(\Delta_M=O(M^{-1})\).  On \(\mathcal J_0\)
the amplitude \(W\) is real-analytic and nonvanishing, so \(\psi\) is
real-analytic and \(\psi''\) is bounded there.  Taylor expansion of \(\psi\) at
\(\theta_{k,M}\) then gives
\[
 \pi+O(\sigma^M)
 =\bigl(M+1-\psi'(\theta_{k,M})\bigr)\Delta_M+O(M^{-2}).
\]
Dividing by \(M+1-\psi'(\theta_{k,M})\) and expanding its reciprocal proves
\eqref{eq:local-strong-clock}; the exponentially small term is
\(o(M^{-3})\).
\end{proof}

For a fixed proper numerator \(N\), apply \Cref{prop:local-strong-clock} with
\(B=B_N\) and \(M=m-\lambda_N\), and write \(W_N,\psi_N\) for the resulting
amplitude and phase.  By \eqref{eq:W-on-g},
\[
 W_N(\theta)=\frac{B_N(\gamma(\theta))}
 {\gamma(\theta)^r g'(\gamma(\theta))},
\]
so \(\psi_N'=\Im(W_N'/W_N)\) is the sum of a denominator-only term and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:numerator-clock-correction}
 \Im\!\left(
  \frac{B_N'(\gamma(\theta))}{B_N(\gamma(\theta))}\,
  \gamma'(\theta)
 \right).
\end{equation}
In the shifted index the leading clock \(\pi/(M+1)\) is therefore
denominator-universal, the numerator entering it only through the reindexing
\(\lambda_N\), while the canonical weight first reaches the local spacing at
order \(M^{-2}\), through the logarithmic derivative
\eqref{eq:numerator-clock-correction}.  \Cref{fig:clock-collapse} shows this
collapse at three indices for the fixed weight \(B(t)=1+t\).

\subsection{Isolated exceptional attractors}\label{subsec:isolated-attractors}

The same residue amplitude has a one-branch manifestation.  On the
Forg\'acs--Tran interval two conjugate roots are co-dominant and their
interference produces the zero bulk.  If instead one denominator root is
strictly dominant, a zero of its residue amplitude attracts a finite packet of
coefficient zeros.  The result is entirely local: unlike the bounded-defect
theorem, it does not require positive-rootedness of \(Q\).  That such an
amplitude zero is a limit of zeros is case~(i) of the Beraha--Kahane--Weiss
theorem \cite{Beraha1975LimitsZeroes}, restated as
\cite[Thm.~1.5(a)]{Sokal2004ChromaticRoots}.  Exponentially
rapid convergence to simple isolated limiting points is familiar in transfer-matrix
examples \cite{SalasSokal2009TransferV}, while Goh, He, and Ricci obtained the
matching count for the Tribonacci pencil \cite[Lems.~8--9]{Goh2009ZeroAttractor}.
The proposition below gives the exact packet multiplicity \(\nu\) and the
spectral-gap rate \(O(\sigma^{M/\nu})\); \Cref{rem:cancellation-meaning}
identifies \(\nu\) with local numerator--denominator intersection
multiplicity.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:isolated-dominant-cancellation}
Let \(r\ge1\), let \(Q\in\R[t]\) satisfy \(Q(0)\ne0\), and let
\(B\in\R[t]\setminus\{0\}\).  Define
\[
 D(t,z)=Q(t)+zt^r,
 \qquad
 F_M(z)=[t^M]\frac{B(t)}{D(t,z)}.
\]
Let \(t_0\in\C^\times\) be a zero of \(B\) of multiplicity \(\nu\ge1\), and set
\[
 z_0=-\frac{Q(t_0)}{t_0^r}.
\]
Assume that \(t_0\) is a simple zero of \(D(\,\cdot\,,z_0)\) and is its unique
minimum-modulus finite zero:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:isolated-dominant-root}
 |t_0|<|t|
 \qquad
 \text{for every other finite zero \(t\) of \(D(t,z_0)\)}.
\end{equation}
Define the local spectral ratio
\begin{equation}\label{eq:local-spectral-ratio}
 \chi_0=
 \max\left(
 \left\{
 \left|\frac{t_0}{t}\right|:
 D(t,z_0)=0,\ t\ne t_0
 \right\}\cup\{0\}
 \right)<1.
\end{equation}
Then there is a neighborhood \(U\) of \(z_0\) such that, for every sufficiently
large \(M\), \(F_M\) has exactly \(\nu\) zeros in \(U\), counted with
multiplicity.  Moreover, for every \(\sigma\in(\chi_0,1)\),
all of these zeros satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:isolated-cancellation-rate}
 |z-z_0|=O\!\left(\sigma^{M/\nu}\right).
\end{equation}
In particular, if \(\nu=1\), then for all sufficiently large \(M\) there is a
unique simple zero \(z_M\) of \(F_M\) near \(z_0\), and \(z_M=z_0+O(\sigma^M)\)
for every \(\sigma\in(\chi_0,1)\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since \(t_0\) is a simple denominator zero, the implicit-function theorem gives
a holomorphic root branch \(t=t(z)\) near \(z_0\) with \(t(z_0)=t_0\) and
\(D(t(z),z)=0\).  Moreover,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dominant-root-derivative}
 t'(z_0)
 =-\frac{\partial_zD(t_0,z_0)}{\partial_tD(t_0,z_0)}
 =-\frac{t_0^r}{\partial_tD(t_0,z_0)}\ne0.
\end{equation}
Put
\[
 \mathcal A(z)=-\frac{B(t(z))}{\partial_tD(t(z),z)}.
\]
Because \(B\) has a zero of order \(\nu\) at \(t_0\) and \(t'(z_0)\ne0\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:isolated-amplitude-order}
 \mathcal A(z)=(z-z_0)^\nu\mathcal V(z),
 \qquad
 \mathcal V(z_0)\ne0.
\end{equation}

Choose once an \(\eta_0\in(\chi_0,1)\).  By
\eqref{eq:isolated-dominant-root}, a radius \(R_0\) can be chosen with
\[
 |t_0|/\eta_0<R_0<|t|
 \quad\text{for every other finite zero \(t\) of \(D(t,z_0)\)},
\]
where the right-hand restriction is vacuous if there is no other finite zero.
Take \(\Gamma_0=\{|t|=R_0\}\).  After shrinking the \(z\)-neighborhood,
\(t(z)\) is the only denominator zero inside \(\Gamma_0\) and
\(|t(z)|/R_0\le\eta_0\).  Hence
\Cref{lem:contour-separation} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:isolated-dominant-expansion}
 t(z)^{M+1}F_M(z)=\mathcal A(z)+O(\eta_0^M)
\end{equation}
uniformly there.  Choose a small disk \(U\) centered at \(z_0\), contained in
that neighborhood, on whose boundary \(\mathcal A\) does not vanish and in
which \(z_0\) is its only zero.  Rouch\'e's theorem applied to
\eqref{eq:isolated-dominant-expansion} shows that \(F_M\) has exactly \(\nu\)
zeros in \(U\) for every sufficiently large \(M\).

Now fix \(\sigma\in(\chi_0,1)\) and choose
\(\eta\in(\chi_0,\sigma)\).  Repeating the same origin-centered separating-circle construction with
\(\eta\) gives, on some neighborhood contained in \(U\),
\[
 t(z)^{M+1}F_M(z)=\mathcal A(z)+O(\eta^M).
\]
On the circle \(|z-z_0|=C_*\eta^{M/\nu}\),
\eqref{eq:isolated-amplitude-order} gives
\(|\mathcal A(z)|\ge cC_*^\nu\eta^M\) for all sufficiently large \(M\), while
the remainder is at most \(C\eta^M\).  Choosing \(C_*\) sufficiently large and
applying Rouch\'e again shows that the shrinking disk contains \(\nu\) zeros.
Since the fixed disk \(U\) contains exactly \(\nu\), these are the same zeros,
and
\[
 |z-z_0|=O\!\left(\eta^{M/\nu}\right)
 =O\!\left(\sigma^{M/\nu}\right).
\]
When \(\nu=1\), the single zero has multiplicity one.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:cancellation-meaning}
The packet multiplicity is intrinsic before reduction.  For the canonical
Laurent restriction of \Cref{lem:laurent-reduction},
\[
 N(t,g(t))=L_N(t)=t^{\lambda_N}B_N(t).
\]
At a nonzero simple denominator root \(t_0\), multiplication by the monomial
\(t^{\lambda_N}\) does not change vanishing order, while
\[
 g'(t_0)=-\frac{\partial_tD(t_0,z_0)}{t_0^r}\ne0.
\]
Thus the denominator curve is smooth and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cancellation-intersection-multiplicity}
 \nu=\operatorname{ord}_{t_0}B_N
 =\operatorname{ord}_{t_0}N(t,g(t))
 =I_{(t_0,z_0)}(N,D),
\end{equation}
the local intersection multiplicity of the numerator and denominator curves.
The proposition therefore says that a uniquely dominant cancellation of
intersection multiplicity \(\nu\) attracts exactly \(\nu\) coefficient zeros.
If \(z_0\notin[0,\infty)\), these are persistent exceptional zeros; the closed
ray is needed, since a packet attracted to \(z_0=0\) may approach it from
inside \((0,\infty)\).
\end{remark}

The exceptional pair in \Cref{fig:decomposition-and-defect}(B) is an exact
instance of \Cref{prop:isolated-dominant-cancellation}.

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:panel-B-attractor}
For \(Q(t)=(1-t)(1-t/2)(1-t/4)\), \(r=1\), and \(N(t,z)=1+z+z^2+t(2-z)\), let
\(N/(Q+zt)=\sum_{m\ge0}P_m(z)t^m\).  Then
\[
 [z^{m+2}]P_m=(-1)^m,
 \qquad \deg P_m=m+2 \qquad(m\ge0).
\]
There is a nonreal algebraic point
\(z_*=-0.5655268358\ldots+1.3674915753\ldots i\) such that, for all
sufficiently large \(m\), \(P_m\) has a unique simple zero \(z_m\) near
\(z_*\) with \(z_m=z_*+O(3^{-m})\),
and the conjugate zero converges at the same rate to \(\overline{z_*}\).
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Coefficient comparison gives
\[
 P_0=z^2+z+1,\quad
 P_1=-z^3+\frac34z^2-\frac14z+\frac{15}{4},\quad
 P_m=-\left(z-\frac74\right)P_{m-1}-\frac78P_{m-2}+\frac18P_{m-3},
\]
with \(P_{-1}=0\).  Thus \(-zP_{m-1}\) is inductively the unique term of
degree \(m+2\), proving the first assertion.

Direct substitution in the canonical Laurent restriction gives
\[
 N(t,g(t))=t^{-2}B(t),
 \qquad
 64B(t)=t^6-22t^5+141t^4-252t^3+548t^2-288t+64.
\]
Thus \(\lambda_N=-2\), and \Cref{lem:laurent-reduction} gives
\(P_m=F_{m+2}\) for all sufficiently large \(m\).  Write \(64B=\mathcal Q+\mathcal H\), with \(\mathcal Q=548t^2-288t+64\) and
\(\mathcal H=t^6-22t^5+141t^4-252t^3\).  On \(|t|=1/2\),
\[
 |\mathcal H(t)|\le\frac{2625}{64}
 <\sqrt{\frac{298424}{137}}\le|\mathcal Q(t)|,
\]
the last inequality following by minimizing
\(|\mathcal Q(\tfrac12e^{i\theta})|^2=35072\cos^2\theta-57888\cos\theta+26065\).
Since the two zeros \((36\pm8i\sqrt{14})/137\) of \(\mathcal Q\) have modulus
\(4/\sqrt{137}<1/2\), Rouch\'e gives exactly two zeros of \(B\) in
\(|t|<1/2\).  They are nonreal: \(64B(t)>0\) termwise on \([-1/2,0]\), while
on \([0,1/2]\)
\[
 \mathcal Q(t)=548\left(t-\frac{36}{137}\right)^2+\frac{3584}{137}
 \ge\frac{3584}{137},\qquad
 \mathcal H(t)\ge \mathcal H(1/2)=-\frac{1495}{64},
\]
the second inequality because the cubic factor in
\(\mathcal H'(t)=2t^2(3t^3-55t^2+282t-378)\) has derivative at least \(917/4\)
on \([0,1/2]\), hence increases there and is bounded above by its value
\(-2003/8\) at \(1/2\); so \(\mathcal H'\le0\) and \(\mathcal H\) decreases.
Hence \(64B\ge24561/8768>0\).  Thus the inner zeros form a simple conjugate pair.
Let \(t_*\) be the upper-half-plane one and set \(z_*=-Q(t_*)/t_*\).

If \(u,v\) are the other denominator zeros at \(z_*\), then \(-8(Q(t)+z_*t)\)
is \(t^3-7t^2+(14-8z_*)t-8\), and Vieta gives
\[
 u+v=7-t_*,\qquad uv=\frac8{t_*}.
\]
Were \(|u|\le3/2\), then \(|v|>32/3\) and hence \(|u+v|>55/6\), contradicting
\(|7-t_*|<15/2\); the same argument applies to \(v\).  Thus \(|u|,|v|>3/2\),
so \(t_*\) is the unique minimum-modulus denominator zero and the local
spectral ratio of \eqref{eq:local-spectral-ratio} is less than
\(\tfrac{1/2}{3/2}=\tfrac13\).  Moreover, \(z_*\notin\R\), since otherwise
\(\overline{t_*}\) would be another denominator zero of modulus \(|t_*|\).
Applying \Cref{prop:isolated-dominant-cancellation} with \(\nu=1\),
\(\sigma=1/3\), and \(P_m=F_{m+2}\) gives \(z_m-z_*=O(3^{-m})\), and
conjugation gives the second zero.

Finally \Cref{rem:cancellation-meaning} gives \(N(t_*,z_*)=0\).  Since
\(N(t,2)=7\), solving \(N(t,z)=0\) for \(t\) and substituting in the
denominator gives
\[
 Q\!\left(\frac{z^2+z+1}{z-2}\right)+z\,\frac{z^2+z+1}{z-2}
 =-\frac{z^6-12z^5+44z^4-44z^3+96z^2-104z+135}{8(z-2)^3}.
\]
Thus \(z_*\) is algebraic; the displayed decimal merely selects the relevant
conjugate root.
\end{proof}

\section{Concluding remarks}\label{sec:conclusion}

Taken together, the results give a single spectral description of what fixed
initial conditions change.  On the denominator curve \(D(t,z)=0\), the
numerator restricts canonically to
\[
 L_N(t)=N(t,g(t))=t^{\lambda_N}B_N(t).
\]
The monomial factor \(t^{\lambda_N}\) produces only an index and degree shift.
The remaining weight \(B_N\) acts at two scales.  Its degree controls the
eventual global discrepancy, while its restriction to the dominant spectral
locus controls the local fine structure.  On a zero-free co-dominant arc its
logarithmic derivative supplies the numerator-dependent term in the first
clock correction \eqref{eq:numerator-clock-correction}; zeros of \(B_N\)
suppress the principal amplitude and, on uniquely dominant branches, produce
cancellation packets whose size is, by \Cref{rem:cancellation-meaning}, the
local numerator--denominator intersection multiplicity.
Thus, apart from the index and degree shift carried by \(\lambda_N\), the
denominator determines the bulk zero law: \Cref{thm:main,prop:equidistribution}
show that the numerator contributes at most a bounded global defect and does
not alter the limiting zero distribution.

The contour-separated expansion is the common perturbative mechanism.  Only
fibers of \(g(t)=-Q(t)/t^r\) whose moduli can compete need to be resolved
individually; every other pole is exponentially negligible.  For a co-dominant
conjugate pair the resulting normal form is oscillatory: globally its sign
changes survive amplitude zeros and endpoint clusters to give the bounded
angular discrepancy \eqref{eq:angular-discrepancy}, while on a zero-free
compact subarc the same normal form sharpens to the phase quantization and
strong-clock law of \Cref{prop:local-strong-clock}.  For a uniquely dominant
branch the normal form is instead a single analytic amplitude plus an
exponentially small remainder, and its zeros produce the exceptional packets
of \Cref{prop:isolated-dominant-cancellation}.  In this sense co-dominance
quantizes the zero bulk, whereas unique dominance localizes cancellations.
The positive-rootedness of \(Q\) supplies the global Forg\'acs--Tran
dominant-pair geometry; the Laurent reduction, contour expansion, local phase
quantization, and isolated-cancellation mechanism themselves are more general.

The order \(O(1)\) of the defect is optimal.  The factorization
\(N(t,z)=R(z)\) gives \(P_m(z)=R(z)H_m(z)\), so an arbitrary fixed collection
of exceptional zeros can persist at every index.  Such zeros need not come
from a fixed factor: \Cref{prop:isolated-dominant-cancellation} and
\Cref{cor:panel-B-attractor} exhibit a second mechanism in which exceptional
zeros converge exponentially to algebraic cancellation points.

The first unresolved issue is therefore quantitative.

\begin{question}\label{q:quantitative}
Do there exist constants \(C_0(Q,r)\) and \(C_1(Q,r)\), depending only on the
admissible denominator, such that for every \(B\in\R[t]\) with \(B(0)\ne0\) and
every \(M\ge0\) with \(F_M\not\equiv0\),
\[
 \#\{\text{zeros of }F_M\text{ outside }I_{Q,r}\}
 \le C_0(Q,r)+C_1(Q,r)\deg B,
\]
counted with multiplicity?
\end{question}

The issue is genuinely all-index rather than one of uniform onset:
\Cref{rem:degree-attainment} gives degree-one weights with degree drops at
arbitrarily large indices, so a positive answer cannot follow merely by making
the onset in \Cref{prop:angular-discrepancy} uniform in \(\deg B\).  The
Laurent reduction introduces no analogous obstruction, since
\Cref{lem:laurent-reduction} bounds both its transient and \(\deg B_N\) in
terms of the numerator bidegrees; a positive answer would therefore bound the
original sequence in terms of those bidegrees and the fixed denominator data.
In the quadratic case \Cref{rem:quadratic-case} gives the stronger reduced
bound \(\deg B+2\) at every index, and for the pencil \(1+s+t+zst\) Luong and
Tran obtain the explicit numerator-degree bound
\(\deg_s\widetilde N+\deg_t\widetilde N+2\deg_z\widetilde N\)
\cite{Luong2022FourTerm}.

The local results suggest a complementary classification problem.

\begin{question}\label{q:spectral-completeness}
Can eventual positive real-rootedness be characterized entirely from the
canonical weight \(B_N\) on the dominant spectral locus?  More precisely, are
the only asymptotic obstructions
\begin{enumerate}
\item zeros of \(B_N\) on a simple uniquely dominant branch over
      \(z_0\notin[0,\infty)\), which produce the exceptional packets of
      \Cref{prop:isolated-dominant-cancellation}; and
\item zeros of the principal amplitude \(W\) on the co-dominant
      Forg\'acs--Tran arc, where the leading two-branch oscillation is
      suppressed, together with a local sign or interlacing condition there?
\end{enumerate}
If so, is all-index positive real-rootedness characterized by these spectral
conditions together with the finitely many pre-reduction coefficient
polynomials?
\end{question}

This is a numerator-sensitive refinement of the operator problem posed by
Forg\'acs and Tran in their Problem~15 \cite{Forgacs2016RationalGenerating}.
Necessary and sufficient conditions of this kind are known for a linear
combination of Chebyshev polynomials with coefficients the powers of \(az+b\)
\cite{Tran2021LinearCombinations}.

Finally, the proof leaves a denominator-side question that is independent of
the particular numerator.

\begin{question}\label{q:denominator-pencils}
Which real denominator pencils \(D(t,z)\) admit a real dominant-pair locus
\(\mathcal L\) such that, for every fixed proper polynomial numerator, all but
\(O(1)\) coefficient zeros lie on \(\mathcal L\), uniformly in the coefficient
index?  Do intrinsic dominant-pair and endpoint-separation conditions suffice?
\end{question}

The first test case is the linear-type denominator
\(D(t,z)=Q_0(t)+zt^rQ_1(t)\), with nonconstant \(Q_1\), studied under
separation hypotheses by Forg\'acs and Tran \cite{Forgacs2020Hyperbolic}.  Here
the canonical restriction is rational rather than polynomial, with fixed poles
at the zeros of \(Q_1\).  Does the bounded-defect theorem survive whenever
those poles remain outside the dominant separating contours, and what local
zero law appears at the first parameter where such a pole meets a dominant
branch or endpoint cluster?
